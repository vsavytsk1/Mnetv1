# Beyond float64: Extended-Precision Numerical Computation, Reproducibility, and How Physics Collaborations Break the ~15.95-Digit Ceiling

## TL;DR
- **IEEE 754 float64 gives ~15.95 decimal digits (53-bit mantissa); to go beyond it you have three tiers — (1) "twice-precision" tricks (double-double ≈32 digits, quad-double ≈64 digits) built from ordinary float64 via error-free transformations; (2) true arbitrary precision (MPFR/GMP, Julia BigFloat); and (3) rigorous error tracking (Arb ball arithmetic, interval arithmetic).** For your browser work the single highest-leverage fix is not more digits at all but *algebraic rearrangement* so cancellation never happens, plus computing convergence rates from exact BigInt identities.
- **Your specific bug is textbook catastrophic cancellation followed by a ratio of two cancelled quantities.** The fix is not float128 — it is to (a) compute the deviation `F_{n+1}/F_n − φ` from its closed form (order `φ⁻²ⁿ`, which never cancels), and (b) never estimate a convergence rate by dividing successive tiny differences once they approach machine epsilon; use the log-slope of a monotone error sequence or a known-order fit instead.
- **The physics collaborations you named split cleanly into "measurement precision" (which does NOT require many float digits) and "computational precision" (which sometimes does).** The genuinely documented cases where float64 is *insufficient* are: pulsar timing (TEMPO2/PINT use 80-bit long double / double-double for nanosecond-over-decades TOAs), long-term solar-system integration (Laskar), and reproducible LHC beam tracking (SixTrack, which used crlibm to get bit-identical results across machines). LIGO's 10⁻²¹ strain and LIGO/CERN reconstruction do **not** need 21 digits.

## Key Findings

1. **The ceiling is exact and quantifiable.** float64 has a 52-bit stored mantissa (53 effective with the implicit bit), giving log₁₀(2⁵³) ≈ 15.95 decimal digits and machine epsilon ε ≈ 2.22×10⁻¹⁶. ECMAScript's `Number` is exactly this type; WebAssembly's only floating types are `f32` and `f64` (IEEE 754 binary32/binary64) — **there is no float128 in WASM**, so the double-precision ceiling is hard in the browser unless you build wider arithmetic yourself.

2. **Double-double / quad-double buys ~32 / ~64 digits cheaply and portably.** Bailey, Hida & Li's QD library represents a number as an unevaluated sum of 2 or 4 float64s (106 / 212 mantissa bits ≈ 32 / 64 digits) using Dekker/Knuth error-free transformations (TwoSum, TwoProduct/FMA). It does *not* extend exponent range (max ≈ 10³⁰⁸). This is the sweet spot for the browser.

3. **True arbitrary precision means MPFR/GMP.** GNU MPFR (correctly-rounded, arbitrary precision, built on GMP) is the gold standard; Arb/FLINT (Fredrik Johansson) adds *rigorous* midpoint-radius "ball" arithmetic that tracks error bounds automatically. Both are available in the browser via `gmp-wasm` (WASM port of GMP+MPFR).

4. **The reproducibility problem is separate from the precision problem — and it is exactly what bit you with three "equal" arctan routes.** IEEE 754 mandates correct rounding only for +, −, ×, ÷, √ (and FMA). Transcendentals (sin, atan, exp, pow, log) are only *recommended* correctly-rounded, so every engine ships a different approximation. This is why your three algebraically identical angle routes differ by ~3.86 ulp on one V8 engine, and why the same code differs across engines/CPUs.

5. **Correctly-rounded libraries now exist and this is the frontier.** The CORE-MATH project (Inria) and Rutgers's RLIBM provide correctly-rounded elementary functions; the older crlibm (ENS Lyon) is what CERN's SixTrack used to make LHC beam-dynamics tracking bit-reproducible across tens of thousands of volunteer machines. IEEE 754-2019 recommends correctly-rounded transcendentals and a 2029 revision may mandate them.

6. **Pulsar timing is the cleanest "float64 is genuinely insufficient" case.** Representing an MJD epoch (~6×10⁴ days ≈ 5.2×10⁹ s) to 1 ns needs ~19 significant digits — beyond float64's ~15.95. TEMPO2 uses 80-bit long double throughout; PINT uses numpy.longdouble and *refuses to run* if the platform can't hit ns precision; Vela.jl uses double-double (Double64) precisely to be hardware-portable.

## Details

### (1) Software methods for extended and arbitrary precision

**Error-free transformations (EFTs) and double-double/quad-double.** The foundational trick is that the rounding error of a float64 addition or multiplication is itself exactly representable in float64, and can be recovered. Knuth's `TwoSum` (6 flops) computes `[x,y]` with `x = fl(a+b)` and `y` the exact roundoff; Dekker's `FastTwoSum` (3 flops) does it when `|a|≥|b|`; `TwoProduct` recovers a product's roundoff in one FMA. Chaining these gives multi-component arithmetic:

- **Double-double (DD):** an unevaluated sum of two float64s → 106 mantissa bits ≈ **~32 decimal digits**, working precision ~2.2×10⁻³² (vs ε≈2.2×10⁻¹⁶ for float64).
- **Quad-double (QD):** four float64s → 212 bits ≈ **~64 decimal digits** (~2.4×10⁻⁶³).

The canonical implementations are David Bailey, Yozo Hida & Xiaoye Li's **QD library** (ARITH-15, 2001) and the branch-free CAMPARY generator. Performance: DD multiply costs roughly the same as a complex multiply; the University of Illinois-Chicago course notes describe DD overhead as "predictable" and comparable to complex arithmetic. **Failure modes:** (i) no extended exponent range — overflow/underflow happen at the same magnitudes as float64; (ii) the QD transcendental cores (sin, atan) have naive range reduction and are inaccurate for large arguments and can even segfault on NaN (documented in the EXPLANIFLOAT paper, arXiv:2503.11884) — you must do range reduction yourself; (iii) DD/QD are *not* correctly rounded, so they don't by themselves solve reproducibility, though they are deterministic given the same primitive ops and no FMA-contraction differences.

**True arbitrary precision.** When you need to *set* the precision:
- **GMP** (integers/rationals, the performance floor everyone builds on), **MPFR** (correctly-rounded arbitrary-precision floats, IEEE-754-like semantics with directed rounding), **MPC** (complex on top of MPFR).
- **Arb / FLINT** (Fredrik Johansson): "ball" (midpoint-radius) arithmetic that carries a rigorous error bound with every value; competitive with or faster than MPFR, and used for rigorous special-function evaluation and even computer-assisted proofs (e.g. cited in Helfgott's ternary Goldbach work). Arb's philosophy — every quantity is `[m ± r]` with a guaranteed enclosure — is *exactly* your "target / current / error / floor" discipline made rigorous.
- **Python:** `mpmath` (pure-Python arbitrary float, or MPFR-backed via gmpy2), `decimal` (base-10, correctly rounded), `fractions.Fraction` (exact rationals), and SymPy for symbolic/exact.
- **Julia:** `BigFloat` (MPFR-backed), `DoubleFloats.jl` (DD/QD), `ArbNumerics.jl` (Arb), `Measurements.jl` (linear error propagation — good for physical uncertainties but *not* rigorous enclosure).
- **C++:** Boost.Multiprecision (unified interface over `__float128`, MPFR, GMP, and its own `cpp_dec_float`/`cpp_bin_float`); **Rust:** `rug` (GMP/MPFR bindings), `astro-float` (pure Rust).

**IEEE 754 binary128 (quad precision).** 113-bit mantissa → 33–36 decimal digits. **Hardware support is rare:** IBM z/Architecture (since z13, and the earlier ESA/390 G5 in 1998) and IBM POWER9 (Power ISA 3.0) have native binary128; SPARC V8/V9 and PA-RISC define it in the ISA; RISC-V has an optional Q extension; **x86, ARM, and mainstream GPUs do not implement it in hardware.** On x86, GCC/Clang's `__float128` + `libquadmath` is software-emulated and roughly an order of magnitude slower for arithmetic and much worse for transcendentals: measurements show scalar `exp`/`log` in quad running ~50–100× slower than double (≈0.6–0.8 vs ~50 MOPS in one benchmark), and Zimmermann's INRIA talk notes glibc quad `exp` averages ~3200 cycles. Whole-application binary128 is often 100–1000× slower than binary64 (per the IBM z13 GEMM study), which is why FPGA/Ozaki-scheme acceleration exists.

**Interval, affine, and ball arithmetic ("validated/certified numerics").** Instead of "more digits," compute a rigorous *enclosure* of the true answer. Classic interval arithmetic `[lo, hi]` can be pessimistic (the "wrapping" and dependency problems); affine arithmetic tracks linear correlations to tighten bounds; Arb's ball arithmetic `[m ± r]` is the modern, efficient form. Warwick Tucker's *Validated Numerics* (Princeton, 2011) is the standard reference. This directly formalizes your ethic: you never print a target as a result; here you never even claim a digit you cannot enclose.

**Exact / symbolic computation — the most important tool for your problem.** For quantities like `arctan(√15 − 2√3)` you can avoid the whole floating mess:
- **Compute exactly in an algebraic number field.** `√3`, `√5`, `φ = (1+√5)/2` all live in `ℚ(√3, √5)`; SymPy/SageMath/Mathematica/Maxima can simplify, prove identities, and produce closed forms. Your three "equal" arctan routes are provably equal as algebraic numbers — a CAS will confirm the identity symbolically, and the ulp differences you saw are purely artifacts of the engine's `Math.atan`/`Math.pow` approximations, not real disagreements.
- **Algebraic rearrangement to kill cancellation.** This is the key technique and it directly repairs your diagnostic. The identity you cited — `F_{n+1}/F_n − φ = (−φ⁻¹)ⁿ/F_n`-type closed form — *never cancels* because you evaluate the small quantity directly rather than as a difference of two ~equal large quantities. Concretely: because `F_n = (φⁿ − ψⁿ)/√5` with `ψ = −φ⁻¹`, the deviation `F_{n+1}/F_n − φ` has an exact closed form of order `φ⁻²ⁿ`; evaluating *that* (in BigInt rationals, exactly) gives you the deviation to full accuracy at every `n`, including `n=36` where your subtractive route is pure noise. The contraction ratio dev_n/dev_{n−1} → −φ⁻² is then a ratio of two *exactly known* quantities, not "dust over dust."
- **Exact rational arithmetic via BigInt numerator/denominator** is superior to float for any quantity that is genuinely rational (Fibonacci ratios `F_{n+1}/F_n`, lattice norms `T = k²+kℓ+ℓ²`, fullerene vertex/edge/face counts). You already do the integer part; extend it to keep ratios as exact `BigInt/BigInt` pairs and only convert to float64 at *display* time.

**Compensated summation and dot products.** For accumulations, error-free transformations give you "twice the working precision for free":
- **Kahan summation** (1965) carries a running compensation term; **Kahan–Babuška–Neumaier** fixes Kahan's failure when the next term is larger than the accumulator; **Klein** (second-order) goes further; **pairwise (cascade) summation** is what NumPy/Julia `sum` use by default.
- **Ogita–Rump–Oishi** (SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 26(6):1955–1988, 2005) `Sum2`/`Dot2` and `SumK`/`DotK` give results "as accurate as if computed in twice (or K-fold) the working precision," ~40% faster than the XBLAS reference. Empirically (AccurateArithmetic.jl benchmarks): naive/pairwise sums become pure noise once the condition number exceeds 1/ε ≈ 10¹⁶; compensated (Neumaier, Ogita–Rump–Oishi) stay accurate until ~1/ε² ≈ 10³².
- **Reproducible/exact BLAS:** ExBLAS and Intel's Conditional Numerical Reproducibility, plus the **Kulisch exact accumulator** (a very wide fixed-point register that makes dot products associative and exactly reproducible). IEEE 754-2019 added *augmented operations* (`augmentedAddition` etc.) precisely to standardize the TwoSum primitive in hardware.

### The reproducibility problem in depth

IEEE 754 requires correct rounding for +, −, ×, ÷, √ and FMA only. Everything transcendental is subject to the **Table Maker's Dilemma** (you can't know in advance how many extra digits you need to round correctly), so the 754 standard only *recommends* correctly-rounded elementary functions. Consequences you're already seeing:

- **Engine-dependent Math.** ECMA-262 §21.3.2 only *recommends* fdlibm for sin/cos/tan and does not mandate any implementation. V8 uses an fdlibm-based implementation; historically Firefox did not use fdlibm for sin/cos/tan (for speed), so results differed. TC39's own May-2015 notes record the deliberate decision *not* to mandate one implementation (Waldemar Horwat: had they frozen one in ES1 "we'd now be stuck with numeric functions inferior in both accuracy and speed"). Athan Reines's write-up "Math in V8 Is Broken" documents that portable numeric JS must currently target "the lowest common denominator."
- **The correctly-rounded library efforts** are the fix: **CORE-MATH** (Inria; `cr_*` functions, MIT-licensed, being adopted by LLVM-libc and evaluated by Meta for custom accelerators), **RLIBM** (Rutgers; formulates correct rounding as a linear-programming problem, targets ≤32-bit types, faster than glibc/Intel libm), **crlibm** (ENS Lyon; the one SixTrack used), and the older IBM libultim/MathLib (whose slow "accurate path" was removed from glibc 2.27+ because a single `pow` call could cost 440,000 cycles). fdlibm (Sun/Oracle) and Intel's libm are the widely-shipped but *not*-correctly-rounded incumbents.
- **The classic float traps:** x87 80-bit extended precision causes **double rounding** and register-spill non-determinism (a value computed in an 80-bit register gives a different result than one spilled to 64-bit memory); FMA **contraction** (`a*b+c` fused vs. not) changes the last bit; and compiler flags `-ffast-math` / `-ffp-contract` / `-mfpmath` silently change results across builds. SixTrack's reproducibility recipe (McIntosh, CERN) is the definitive checklist: force expression evaluation order with parentheses, disable x87 extended precision (use SSE2), use crlibm for elementary functions, use David Gay's correctly-rounded I/O, implement `a**b` as `exp(b*log(a))`, and **disable FMA**.

### (2) The browser / JavaScript specifically

**Your options beyond `Number`:**
- **BigInt** (built-in, exact, unbounded integers) — you already use it. This is your best tool; extend it to exact rationals (BigInt/BigInt) and to fixed-point (BigInt scaled by 10ᵏ or 2ᵏ).
- **decimal.js** (~10M weekly downloads; the most feature-rich: arbitrary-precision decimal with trig/log/exp, basis for the Decimal128 proposal) — best when you need transcendentals at high precision in pure JS.
- **bignumber.js** (same author, MikeMcl; base 2–16, correctly-rounded sqrt, ~8 KB) and **big.js** (~6 KB, decimal only, add/sub/mul/div, no trig) — lighter, no transcendentals.
- **gmp-wasm** (daninet): arbitrary-precision Integer/Rational/**Float** backed by **GMP + MPFR** compiled to WebAssembly; the WASM binary is bundled as base64; supports browsers, workers, Node, Deno; sometimes faster than native BigInt. **This is the way to get true MPFR correctly-rounded transcendentals in the browser.** Maturity: usable, LGPL, low-hundreds of npm dependents (less battle-tested than decimal.js).
- Note there is no widely-used, mature WASM port of **Arb** specifically; if you want rigorous ball arithmetic in the browser today you'd likely wrap MPFR (gmp-wasm) and track your own radius, or port Arb yourself.

**Exact rational arithmetic in JS.** For Fibonacci pairs, hexagonal norms, and fullerene counts — all exact — keep everything as BigInt (integers) or BigInt/BigInt (rationals). Convergence *ratios* like `F_{n+1}/F_n` are exact rationals; the deviation from φ is best evaluated from the closed form, in exact arithmetic, then converted to float64 only for the display line. This eliminates your cancellation entirely because you never subtract two nearly-equal float64s.

**WASM ceiling.** WebAssembly's numeric types are `i32/i64/f32/f64` plus a 128-bit SIMD vector (`v128`) that packs 2×f64 or 4×f32 lanes — **f64 is the hard scalar ceiling**; there is no binary128. A half-precision (f16) proposal exists (for ML/graphics), moving *down*, not up. So higher precision in WASM = software (DD/QD, or GMP/MPFR compiled in), exactly as on x86. SIMD can *speed up* DD/QD but doesn't raise the ceiling.

**Can you work around implementation-defined Math?** Yes, three ways: (1) ship your own correctly-rounded implementations (port CORE-MATH/RLIBM/crlibm, or use MPFR via gmp-wasm) so results are engine-independent; (2) use `Math.fround` to force float32 rounding where you deliberately want a reproducible 32-bit result; (3) compute in BigInt fixed-point for the quantities that must be bit-reproducible, converting to float64 only for display. For your target/current/error panels, the cleanest is: **compute the target and the current value in exact/high precision, subtract in high precision, and only round to float64 for the on-screen digits** — so the "error" is a true error, not a cancellation artifact.

### (3) Hardware and exotic number formats

**Posits / unums (John Gustafson).** A posit has sign/regime/exponent/fraction with a *tapered* accuracy (more precision near ±1, less at extremes), plus the "quire" (a Kulisch-style exact accumulator). **Claims:** better accuracy per bit than IEEE floats, simpler hardware, higher "POPS." **Adoption/criticism (contested):** advocates (posithub; IEEE Spectrum Dec-2022 reporting a Complutense-Madrid posit core with up to 4-orders-of-magnitude accuracy gain on a basic task) vs. skeptics — de Dinechin et al.'s "Evaluating the hardware cost of the posit number system" (FPL 2019) argues the accuracy comparisons are often unfair and the exception handling (no separate ±∞/NaN) is a real defect for drop-in replacement. **Status:** a Posit Standard (2022) exists; there are FPGA/ASIC prototypes (PACoGen, Complutense core) and research interest, but **no mainstream CPU/GPU ships posits**. Treat posit superiority claims as contested, largely from software emulation and favorable benchmarks.

**The opposite trend (lower precision for ML): bfloat16, FP8, TF32.** The economically dominant hardware trend is *down*: bfloat16 (8-bit exponent, 7-bit mantissa), NVIDIA TF32 (19-bit, ~10-bit mantissa), and FP8/FP4 for transformers. This matters for you because it means **high-precision hardware is getting relatively scarcer and more segmented**, not more available.

**GPUs: usually hurt for high precision.** Consumer NVIDIA cards deliberately cripple FP64: the NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 (Blackwell GB202, launched January 2025) does 104.8 TFLOPS FP32 but only **1.637 TFLOPS FP64 — a 64:1 ratio** (per TechPowerUp-class specs); the FP64:FP32 ratio has widened steadily since Fermi (2010) as deliberate market segmentation. Data-center parts are the exception: the A100 does 19.5 TFLOPS FP64 (via FP64 tensor cores), and the H100 delivers ~60 TFLOPS FP64 (about 3× the A100). Shader/WebGPU contexts are typically float32-only. So GPUs help only if you have data-center silicon *and* your problem is FP64-bound; for extended precision beyond FP64 you're back to software (DD/QD on the GPU, e.g. GQD/CAMPARY).

**Dedicated high-precision hardware.** The Kulisch **exact accumulator** (a very wide fixed-point register spanning the full float exponent range) makes summation/dot-products associative and exactly reproducible; it reappears as the posit "quire." FPGA binary128 GEMM (≈90 GFlops, ~147× a 20-thread CPU) and the Ozaki scheme (turning high-precision matmul into many float64 DGEMMs) are the practical routes when you truly need quad in bulk.

### (4) The physics collaborations — measurement vs. computational precision

**The crucial distinction first:** a *measurement* precision like LIGO's 10⁻²¹ strain, or a nanosecond timing residual, is a **dynamic range**, not a demand for that many *digits in a single float*. You need many float digits only when a *computation* accumulates or cancels enough to threaten the 15.95-digit budget. The named collaborations split accordingly.

**Pulsar timing arrays (NANOGrav, EPTA, IPTA) — the cleanest genuine case.** This is the best-documented example of float64 being *insufficient*. Pulsar timing represents times of arrival (TOAs) as Modified Julian Dates (MJD ≈ 6×10⁴ days ≈ 5.2×10⁹ s) but needs ~1 ns precision — a dynamic range of ~5.2×10¹⁸, i.e. **~19 significant decimal digits**, beyond float64's ~15.95. The NANOGrav **PINT documentation** states the problem in a directly checkable way: `(10*u.year*np.finfo(float).eps).to(u.ns)` returns `70.07194824 ns`, and "the smallest increment python's floating-point can cope with is about 70 nanoseconds — not enough for accurate pulsar timing work!" (float64 machine epsilon over a decade-long epoch). Solutions:
- **TEMPO2** (Hobbs, Edwards & Manchester 2006, MNRAS 369:655; Edwards, Hobbs & Manchester 2006, MNRAS 372:1549) implements the timing model "at the 1-ns level of precision (a factor of ∼100 more precise than previously obtainable)"; the manual states "tempo2 reads all parameters with 'long double' precision" and uses 80- or 128-bit long double, warning that some systems (e.g. Macs) provide only 64-bit long double. Where long double is inadequate it falls back to gcc `__float128`.
- **PINT** (Luo, Ransom, Demorest et al. 2021, ApJ 911:45, arXiv:2012.00074) uses `numpy.longdouble` (= C long double, 80-bit hardware on Intel, ~19 digits, machine ε ≈ 1.084×10⁻¹⁹) and **refuses to run** if the platform's long double can't support ns MJD precision (its docs note 64-bit MSVC fails, and that Arm64/POWER9/POWER7 use software/hardware quad or double-double). PINT reports its longdouble MJD precision as "roughly 0.7 ns."
- **Vela.jl** (Susobhanan 2025, arXiv:2412.15858) deliberately uses **Double64** (double-double, sum of two Float64s per Dekker 1971, ~31 digits) *because* it is hardware-portable, sidestepping long double's architecture dependence; it applies extended precision only to `TOA.value` and pulse phase/rotational frequency, leaving everything else Float64 for speed. The residual differences between PINT and Vela.jl (~few ns) are attributed to "the difference in the representation of extended precision numbers" (80-bit long double vs double-double). This is a real-world illustration of *your* exact concern.
- The scientific payoff: the **NANOGrav 15-year dataset** (Agazie et al. 2023, ApJL 951:L8, arXiv:2306.16213) reported evidence for a Hellings–Downs-correlated nanohertz gravitational-wave background. Quantitatively: a frequentist weighted-sum-of-correlations statistic yields p = 5×10⁻⁵ to 1.9×10⁻⁴ (≈3.5σ–4σ); the Bayesian analysis favors the correlated model over independent-noise "with a Bayes factor in excess of 10¹⁴" and over an uncorrelated common-spectrum process "with Bayes factors of 200–1000"; the inferred strain amplitude is 2.4₋₀.₆⁺⁰·⁷ × 10⁻¹⁵ at a reference frequency of 1 yr⁻¹, over a 16.03-yr total baseline. The companion dataset paper (arXiv:2306.16217) describes 68 millisecond pulsars observed 2004 July–2020 August with Arecibo, the GBT and the VLA (67 with ≥3-yr baseline used in the GW analysis), on the TT(BIPM2019) timescale with the JPL DE440 ephemeris — all only possible because individual TOAs are modeled at ~ns precision over ~15-year spans.

**CERN / LHC — reproducibility, not extra digits.** SixTrack simulates proton trajectories for up to ~10⁶ turns around the LHC ring; because the motion is chaotic, a 1-ULP difference "grows exponentially" and completely changes the outcome. The problem was that different CPUs, OSes, and libms gave different last-bit results, breaking the LHC@home volunteer-computing model (tens of thousands of heterogeneous Windows/Linux machines). CERN's solution (Eric McIntosh et al.) was **bit-level reproducibility via crlibm** (correctly-rounded elementary functions from ENS Lyon). Per CERN's EP-SFT group, SixTrack "has been engineered to give the exact same results after millions of operations on several, very different computer platforms … The code relies on the crlibm library, careful arrangement of parenthesis, dedicated input/output and selected compilation flags … to provide identical results on different platforms and operating systems." Note this is float64 throughout — the fix was *determinism*, not more digits. (Lattice QCD, ROOT numerics, and event reconstruction generally live comfortably in float64/float32; lattice QCD even exploits mixed/lower precision with double-precision correction.)

**NASA JPL ephemerides & long-term solar-system integration.** The JPL DE440/DE441 ephemerides (Park et al. 2021, AJ) are produced by numerically integrating and fitting; DE441 spans years −13,200 to +17,191 with planets agreeing with DE440 to within ~1 meter. Long-term integrations are where precision bites: the solar system is **chaotic** with a Lyapunov time ≈ 5 Myr (Laskar; Sussman & Wisdom), so tiny numerical errors amplify exponentially, and 100-Myr-plus integrations (Laskar's work on Mercury's eccentricity; Zeebe's ZB18a, based on DE431, whose divergence from a DE441-based solution sets in at ~66 Ma) are limited by both step-size error and roundoff. Practitioners use symplectic integrators (Wisdom–Holman), compensated/extended summation for the accumulation of billions of tiny time steps, and carefully controlled arithmetic; Laskar's stability studies are the canonical celestial-mechanics extended-precision case.

**LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA — measurement precision ≠ computational digits.** The 10⁻²¹ strain sensitivity is a signal-to-noise/dynamic-range statement, not a 21-digit float requirement; matched filtering and template banks run in float32/float64 (recent differentiable-waveform work runs on GPUs in reduced precision). The precision that *does* matter is **waveform phase accuracy accumulated over many inspiral cycles**: Mark Miller (gr-qc/0502087) showed the accuracy required for numerical-relativity templates is orders of magnitude tighter than then-current simulations, motivating higher-order finite-difference and **spectral methods** (the SXS collaboration's SpEC uses spectral methods precisely for exponential convergence in phase). Waveform agreement is measured by noise-weighted overlap (match), and NR waveforms are ~99% accurate for space-based detectors. This is a *discretization-accuracy* problem (choose a better method / finer grid) more than a *float-digit* problem.

**Other documented cases.** VLBI, atomic-clock comparisons, and CODATA fundamental-constant least-squares adjustments all involve careful error budgets but generally live in float64. GPS/timing (including LIGO's GPS timestamps) needs precise *time* representation (the same MJD/leap-second issue as pulsars) but that's a representation choice, not a per-operation digit crisis.

**Cautionary tales (kept separate — these are float *misuse*, not frontier precision).** *Ariane 5 Flight 501 (1996):* a 64-bit float horizontal-velocity value was converted to a 16-bit signed integer, overflowed, and destroyed the rocket — a *range/conversion* bug. *Patriot missile (1991, Dhahran):* time was tracked in tenths of a second in a 24-bit fixed-point register; 0.1 is not exactly representable in binary, and the accumulated drift over ~100 hours (~0.34 s) caused a tracking-gate miss and 28 deaths — a *representation + accumulation* bug. *Vancouver Stock Exchange index (1982):* truncation (not rounding) on every recalculation drove the index from 1000 to ~520 over ~22 months — a *rounding-direction* bug. All three are about representation/conversion/rounding discipline — the same discipline behind your "never print a target as a result" ethic.

### (5) The mathematics of knowing when you've hit the wall

**The canonical reference is Nicholas J. Higham, *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms* (SIAM, 2nd ed. 2002).** Its Chapter 1 is the exact toolkit for your situation: §1.6 conditioning, §1.7 cancellation, §1.9 computing the sample variance (the classic cancellation example), §1.13 increasing the precision, §1.14 "cancellation of rounding errors," and §1.17 "rounding errors are not random." Key concepts:

- **Condition number** κ measures how much the *problem* amplifies input perturbations; **backward error** measures how far the *computed answer* is from being the exact answer to a nearby problem. Rule of thumb: forward error ≲ condition number × backward error. If κ ≈ 10ᵏ you lose about k digits regardless of algorithm — that's an intrinsic wall, not a bug.
- **Catastrophic cancellation** is subtracting two nearly-equal quantities: the leading digits cancel and previously-invisible rounding error is promoted to the leading position. Higham's crucial nuance: cancellation is only harmful when the operands *already carry error*; subtracting exact inputs (or a well-ordered `x + (y − z)`) is harmless. **Your bug is the harmful kind twice over:** `dev_n = F_{n+1}/F_n − φ` cancels once (two ~equal floats), then `dev_n/dev_{n−1}` divides one cancelled quantity by another.
- **A-priori floor estimate:** the achievable absolute error floor is roughly `ε × scale`, where `scale` is the magnitude of the largest intermediate. For your deviation, once `dev_n ~ φ⁻²ⁿ` drops near `ε·φ ≈ 10⁻¹⁶`, you have zero good digits left — which is exactly why accuracy peaks around n≈16 (about 7 good digits, since φ⁻²ⁿ ≈ ε there) and is garbage by n≈36. Because `|dev_n| ≈ C·φ⁻²ⁿ` and 2·log₁₀φ ≈ 0.418 digits lost per step, the number of trustworthy digits ≈ 15.95 − 0.418·n, crossing zero near n ≈ 16–17 — precisely your observation. This is *predictable a priori*.
- **Empirical precision-loss detection:** rigorous *running error analysis* (Higham §3.3), or stochastic methods — **CESTAC / Discrete Stochastic Arithmetic** implemented in **CADNA** (perturbs the rounding mode, runs 3 samples, reports the number of significant digits and flags "computational zeros"); **Monte Carlo Arithmetic** implemented in **Verificarlo** (Denis et al.) and **Verrou** (perturbs at a chosen virtual precision, no recompilation). These *empirically* tell you how many digits of a real program's output are trustworthy — the automated version of your target/current/error panel.

**Your specific pathology: estimating a convergence rate from ratios of successive differences.** This is a known trap. The observed order of convergence is standardly computed as `p = log(|f₃−f₂|/|f₂−f₁|) / log(r)` from *three* iterates — but this is only valid while the differences are *well above* the roundoff floor (the "asymptotic range"). Once the differences approach ε·scale, the ratio becomes noise/noise. Best practices from numerical analysis:
- **Aitken's Δ² / Steffensen** accelerate linearly-convergent sequences but are *notoriously unstable near convergence* precisely because they divide differences of nearly-equal numbers (ScienceDirect: the accelerated sequence "does not converge quadratically" and such methods "do not always work"). Do not use it to *measure* the rate once you're near the floor.
- **Richardson extrapolation** works only when there is a genuine asymptotic expansion with a single direction of convergence (Francis Bach's analysis: it helps for monotone convergence, is useless for oscillating iterates, and "requires a specific direction of asymptotic convergence"). Your ratio → −φ⁻² *oscillates in sign* (the `(−φ⁻¹)ⁿ` factor), which is exactly the case where naive extrapolation fails.
- **The correct approach for you:** (a) compute the deviation from the *exact closed form* in BigInt/rational arithmetic so it never hits the floor; (b) estimate the rate as the **slope of a linear fit to log|dev_n| vs n** over the window where `dev_n` is above the floor (`n ≲ 16`), which robustly recovers the theoretical `−2·log₁₀φ⁻¹` (i.e. the ratio −φ⁻²); (c) if you must use ratios, take them of the *exact* deviations, and explicitly *stop* (or grey out the diagnostic) once `|dev_n| < c·ε·scale`, displaying "below floor" instead of a garbage ratio — the honest extension of "never print a target as a result."

## Recommendations

**Staged, concrete, for a browser-based live target/current/error simulation:**

1. **Immediately (no new dependencies): kill the cancellation algebraically.** Replace `dev_n = F_{n+1}/F_n − φ` (float subtraction) with the exact closed form evaluated in BigInt rationals: since `F_{n+1}/F_n − φ` is of order `φ⁻²ⁿ` with an exact `−ψⁿ⁺¹/(√5·F_n)`-type numerator/denominator, compute those as exact BigInt quantities and divide, converting to float64 only for display. This alone fixes the n≈36 garbage. **Benchmark that would change this:** if you find you also need √5 or φ to more than ~15 digits for the *display* line, add a high-precision constant (from decimal.js or gmp-wasm).

2. **Fix the convergence-rate diagnostic.** Stop computing `dev_n/dev_{n−1}` on floats. Instead fit `log|dev_n|` (exact deviations) linearly against `n` over the pre-floor window and report the slope; separately verify it equals the theoretical `−2 log₁₀ φ⁻¹`. **Gate the display:** when `|dev_n| < 8·ε·|scale|` (Higham-style floor with a safety factor), render the diagnostic as "below numerical floor" rather than a number. This *is* your "never print a target" ethic applied to error bars.

3. **Fix the reproducibility of angles/transcendentals.** For any quantity that must be bit-identical across engines (your three arctan routes), do one of: (a) compute it once in exact/symbolic form and hard-code the high-precision constant; (b) route it through **decimal.js** (pure-JS, deterministic, has atan) or **gmp-wasm** (MPFR, correctly rounded) so V8/JSC/SpiderMonkey all agree; (c) if you keep native `Math`, *document* that atan/pow/exp are engine-approximated (±few ulp) and never use their last bits in a diagnostic. Choosing `atan2(√3, 2φ+1)` over `atan(√15−2√3)` is worthwhile only if you've verified which is better-conditioned — but the real fix is (a)/(b).

4. **Adopt an explicit "floor" everywhere, à la Arb.** For every displayed quantity, carry a companion error estimate `ε·scale` (or a true `[m ± r]` if you use interval logic). Show target, current, and *error-vs-floor*: if error ≈ floor you've converged to machine limits; if error ≫ floor the algorithm (not precision) is the bottleneck. This makes "hitting the wall" visible and honest.

5. **If/when you need genuinely more digits** (not just anti-cancellation): use **gmp-wasm** for MPFR-backed arbitrary precision with correctly-rounded transcendentals — this is the only browser route to *both* more digits *and* reproducibility. Reserve **decimal.js** for lighter needs. Do **not** reach for float128 (unavailable in WASM) or GPUs (float32/segmented FP64).

6. **Reference shelf to follow:** Higham, *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms* (2002); Muller et al., *Handbook of Floating-Point Arithmetic*; Shewchuk 1997 (adaptive predicates, for the EFT primitives); Ogita–Rump–Oishi 2005 (compensated sum/dot); Bailey/Hida/Li QD papers; the CORE-MATH and RLIBM project pages; Fredrik Johansson's Arb papers (arXiv:1611.02831).

## Caveats
- **Established fact vs. trade-off vs. contested:** The digit counts (15.95 / 32 / 64 / 33–36 / 19), the IEEE-754 correct-rounding scope, WASM's f64 ceiling, and the pulsar/SixTrack/ephemeris cases are established and cited. The DD/QD "predictable overhead" and "twice-precision until κ≈10³²" claims are practitioner-benchmark trends, not proofs. **Posit superiority is genuinely contested** — advocacy is largely from software emulation and favorable task selection; mainstream hardware has not adopted it.
- **Performance numbers are hardware/compiler-specific:** quad-precision slowdowns (50–1000×), GPU FP64:FP32 ratios (up to 64:1 consumer, e.g. RTX 5090's 104.8 vs 1.637 TFLOPS), and library speeds vary by platform and version; treat them as order-of-magnitude.
- **The a-priori "n≈16 peak, n≈36 garbage" derivation** assumes `dev_n ≈ C·φ⁻²ⁿ` and float64 relative error ε≈2.2×10⁻¹⁶; the exact crossover depends on the constant C and your `scale`, but the *shape* (peak then decay) is a certain consequence of dividing dust by dust.
- **LIGO/CERN "don't need many digits" is a claim about the dominant workloads**; specific sub-computations (NR waveform phase, symplectic accumulation) do stress accuracy and are handled by better *methods* (spectral, symplectic, compensated summation) rather than wider floats. I did not find a documented case of LIGO production analysis requiring beyond-float64 arithmetic, whereas for pulsar timing and long-term ephemerides such cases are explicit.
- **The NANOGrav result is "evidence for" (≈3.5σ–4σ / Bayes factor >10¹⁴ vs independent noise), not a >5σ "discovery"-level claim**, and the Vela.jl "difference in representation of extended precision numbers" explanation is the authors' stated likely cause ("we suspect"), not a proven one.
