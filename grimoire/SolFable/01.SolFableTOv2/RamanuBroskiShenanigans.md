# Ramanujan's Latexium: A Grimoire of Compressions and Their Modern Thefts

## TL;DR
- Ramanujan's corpus (~3,900 notebook entries, ~600 "Lost Notebook" entries, 37 papers, four letters to Hardy) is now essentially fully adjudicated: Bruce Berndt examined 3,254 notebook results, the vast majority true, a minority prior rediscoveries, and a small number false; as of the mid-2020s Berndt states there are no remaining unproven claims. The most "algorithmically usable" compressions are the 1/π series, the Rademacher partition series, mock theta functions, Ramanujan graphs, and the Rogers–Ramanujan identities.
- The clearest information-theoretic "compressions" are his π series (Chudnovsky's descendant yields ~14 correct digits per term and powers every world record, including y-cruncher) and the Hardy–Ramanujan–Rademacher formula (an infinite combinatorial object, p(n), collapsed into a convergent closed series). His congruences pack infinitely many divisibility facts into three symbols each.
- The "thefts" by physics and CS are real and load-bearing: mock modular forms count black-hole microstates (Dabholkar–Murthy–Zagier) and drive Mathieu/umbral moonshine; Ramanujan graphs are provably optimal expanders underpinning error-correcting codes and derandomization; Rogers–Ramanujan identities solved Baxter's hard-hexagon model; the tau conjecture seeded the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture in the Langlands program; and −1/12 (zeta-regularization) fixes the 26 dimensions of bosonic string theory.

## Key Findings

**The corpus is finite, mapped, and now essentially closed.** Ramanujan left three notebooks (~3,900 results, compiled 1903–1914, virtually all without proof), the "Lost Notebook" (~600 results from his final year, rediscovered by George Andrews in the Wren Library, Cambridge, in spring 1976), 37 published papers, and four letters to Hardy (16 January 1913; 27 February 1913; 17 April 1913; 22 January 1914) plus the "deathbed" letter of 12 January 1920. Bruce Berndt spent 1977 onward proving/annotating the notebooks across five volumes (1985–1998); by his count 3,254 results were examined. Andrews and Berndt then produced five volumes on the Lost Notebook (2005–2018). In 2020 Berndt told John Baez: "To the best of my knowledge, there are no claims or conjectures remaining."

**Ramanujan's genius was a compression engine, but not infallible.** His prime-counting work was genuinely wrong — Hardy: "Ramanujan's theory of primes was vitiated by his ignorance of the theory of functions of a complex variable... many of the actual results were false" — because he effectively assumed the Riemann zeta function had no complex zeros. Some notebook entries were false or were rediscoveries of Euler, Jacobi, and others. This "failure ledger" matters for a grimoire that treats identities as executable.

## Details

### 1. THE CORPUS ITSELF

**The three notebooks.** Compiled roughly 1903–1914 in India, they contain almost 3,900 results (Berndt's editing covered 3,254 across five volumes), overwhelmingly without proof. The second notebook is a revised/enlarged edition of the first; the third is short. G.N. Watson and B.M. Wilson began editing them in the late 1920s but abandoned the effort after a decade. The Tata Institute of Fundamental Research published an unedited photostat edition in 1957.

- **(a) What it states:** thousands of identities across q-series, modular equations, continued fractions, integrals, theta functions, elliptic functions, and number theory.
- **(b) Proof status:** Berndt (with Andrews and others) proved or referenced every entry across *Ramanujan's Notebooks, Parts I–V* (Springer, 1985, 1989, 1991, 1994, 1998). Where results were previously known, references are given; otherwise complete proofs are supplied.
- **(c) Compression reading:** the notebooks are a lossy-but-dense encoding of results Ramanujan derived by "mingled argument, intuition and induction" (Hardy) — the proofs (the "decompression algorithm") were largely missing and had to be reconstructed by the community over ~40 years.
- **(d) The "steal":** the notebooks are a mine that modern researchers still quarry; the Lost Notebook alone launched the mock-modular revolution.

**Fractions proven / rediscovered / wrong.** No exact partition is published, but the qualitative breakdown is well documented: the large majority were true and many were new; a substantial minority had been found earlier (Berndt notes Chapter 24 on primes is "mostly not new," and much of the special-functions material "little is new"); and a small number were false — most prominently the prime-counting claims. Littlewood and others noted the letters contained results that were "profoundly original," some "well known," and some "incorrect as stated."

**The Lost Notebook journey (documented).** After Watson's death in 1965, J.M. Whittaker found the loose sheets amid Watson's disordered papers (reportedly about to be incinerated); Whittaker and R.A. Rankin sent them to the Trinity College Wren Library on 26 December 1968. George Andrews, following a suggestion by Lucy Slater, rediscovered them there in spring 1976. Narosa published a facsimile on 22 December 1987 (Ramanujan's centenary). Berndt: the discovery "caused roughly as much stir... as the discovery of Beethoven's tenth symphony." The last remaining Lost-Notebook claim was proved by Berndt, Junxian Li, and Alexandru Zaharescu around 2019 (an identity involving a combination of Bessel functions).

### 2. THE π SERIES AND COMPUTATION

**Ramanujan's 1914 paper** "Modular Equations and Approximations to π" (*Quarterly Journal of Mathematics* 45) listed 17 rapidly convergent series for 1/π without proof. The celebrated one:

1/π = (2√2 / 9801) · Σ (4n)!(1103 + 26390n) / [(n!)⁴ · 396⁴ⁿ].

- **(a) What it states:** a hypergeometric series for 1/π. It is the N = 58 case of a general family now called Ramanujan–Sato series, tied to the class invariant/singular modulus g₅₈ (level-58 modular forms).
- **(b) Proof status:** the first term alone gives 1/π ≈ 0.31830988618… correct to about 10 decimal places. The full series is linearly convergent with rate 1/99⁴, so it "adds nearly eight decimal digits per term, since 99⁴ ≈ 10⁸" (Borwein-brothers survey, arXiv:1802.07558). William Gosper used it in 1985 to compute 17,526,100 digits — before it was proved. Jonathan and Peter Borwein proved all 17 of Ramanujan's series in 1987 (*Pi and the AGM*, Wiley, 1987).
- **(c) Compression:** ~8 correct decimal digits of π per term — an entire transcendental constant streamed from a three-integer kernel (1103, 26390, 396).
- **(d) The steal — Chudnovsky (1988):** David and Gregory Chudnovsky derived
1/π = 12 Σ (−1)ᵏ (6k)!(13591409 + 545140134k) / [(3k)!(k!)³ · 640320^(3k+3/2)],
equivalently π = 426880√10005 / Σ(...). Each term yields ~14.18 correct digits (log₁₀(1/μ) ≈ 14.1816). The constant 640320 is tied to the Heegner number 163 (640320³ + 744 ≈ e^(π√163)). Combined with binary splitting, this is the basis of essentially every modern world-record π computation. The 100-trillion-digit run (Google Cloud, Emma Haruka Iwao, announced 8 June 2022) used "y-cruncher v0.7.8, by Alexander J. Yee; Algorithm: Chudnovsky algorithm" on an n2-highmem-128 node, taking 157 days, 23 h, 31 min. The record has since advanced repeatedly (per Wikipedia/HandWiki "Chudnovsky algorithm"): 62.8 trillion (14 August 2021), 100 trillion (21 March 2022), 105 trillion (14 March 2024), 202 trillion (28 June 2024, StorageReview via y-cruncher), and 314 trillion digits (23 November 2025). Higher-order analogues exist: the Berndt–Chan series yields about 74 digits/term (log₁₀(−1/J₃₃₁₅) ≈ 75.3178, per arXiv:2403.07291), and a 2024 extension (arXiv:2403.07291) yields about 153 digits/term, superseding the earlier Bagis–Glasser series (~110 digits/term).
- **The AGM connection:** the Borweins' *Pi and the AGM* ties these to the arithmetic–geometric mean and the quadratically/quartically convergent Gauss–Legendre–Brent–Salamin and Borwein algorithms, which double or quadruple correct digits per iteration and are used for verification runs.

### 3. PARTITIONS AND CONGRUENCES

**The Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotic (1918)**, "Asymptotic Formulae in Combinatory Analysis" (*Proc. London Math. Soc.*), gave p(n) ~ e^(π√(2n/3)) / (4n√3), and in the process **invented the circle method**, exploiting the near-modularity of the generating function (the Dedekind eta function).

- **(c) Compression:** the infinite, irregular sequence p(n) — counting all partitions — is captured by a single analytic expression with exponentially small relative error.
- **Rademacher (1937/38):** Hans Rademacher perfected the circle method into an *exactly convergent* series, p(n) = (1/π√2) Σ_{k≥1} √k A_k(n) · d/dn[ sinh((π/k)√(2/3)(n−1/24)) / √(n−1/24) ], where A_k(n) is a Kloosterman-type sum. This is the ultimate compression: an exact, computable closed form for an infinite combinatorial object. (Selberg independently arrived at the same formula in 1937.)
- **The three congruences:** p(5n+4) ≡ 0 (mod 5); p(7n+5) ≡ 0 (mod 7); p(11n+6) ≡ 0 (mod 11). Ramanujan gave analytic proofs of the first two; Hardy derived all three from his unpublished work. Watson and Atkin extended these to arbitrary powers of 5, 7, 11.
  - **Compression:** each congruence packs infinitely many divisibility facts (one for every n) into a handful of symbols.
- **Dyson's rank and crank:** Freeman Dyson (1944) defined the *rank* (largest part minus number of parts) and conjectured it splits the partitions of 5n+4 and 7n+5 into equal classes mod 5 and 7 (proved by Atkin & Swinnerton-Dyer, 1954). He predicted a "crank" to explain all three; found by George Andrews and Frank Garvan (1988). The rank generating function became an early, important example of a mock modular form (Bringmann–Ono). Karl Mahlburg's 2005 paper on the Andrews–Garvan–Dyson crank won the first PNAS Paper of the Year prize.
- **Ono and all primes:** Ken Ono (2000) proved infinite families of partition congruences exist for every prime ℓ ≥ 5 via the p-adic theory of half-integral-weight modular forms; Ahlgren–Ono extended this to every modulus coprime to 6. Ahlgren–Boylan proved 5, 7, 11 are the *only* primes admitting congruences of the original simple shape.
- **(d) Circle-method descendants:** the circle method became the workhorse of additive analytic number theory — Waring's problem, and (via Hardy–Littlewood and later Vinogradov) the ternary Goldbach problem (every large odd number is a sum of three primes; fully resolved by Helfgott).

### 4. MOCK THETA FUNCTIONS

**The deathbed letter (12 January 1920)** introduced "mock theta functions," giving 17 examples in "orders" 3, 5, and 7. Ramanujan wrote: "I discovered very interesting functions recently which I call 'Mock' ϑ-functions... they enter into mathematics as beautifully as the ordinary theta functions."

- **(a) What they state:** q-series that mimic the near-modularity of theta functions at roots of unity but are not themselves modular.
- **(b) Proof status / the 80-year mystery:** for decades the objects lacked a unifying definition. Sander Zwegers' 2002 Utrecht PhD thesis (advised by Zagier) showed they are the holomorphic parts of weight-1/2 harmonic weak Maass forms, whose non-holomorphic parts are period integrals of weight-3/2 unary theta functions. Bringmann and Ono (2006 onward) built the general theory, proving congruences and exact formulas for their coefficients. Michael Griffin, Ken Ono, and Larry Rolen, "Ramanujan's mock theta functions," *Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA* 110 (2013), no. 15, 5765–5768 (27 March 2013), proved that "Ramanujan's examples do indeed satisfy his original definition."
- **(c) Compression:** a mock theta function encodes an entire non-modular q-series' asymptotics via a "shadow" — add one explicit correction term and modular symmetry is restored.
- **(d) The steal:**
  - **Black-hole entropy:** Dabholkar, Murthy, and Zagier, "Quantum Black Holes, Wall Crossing, and Mock Modular Forms" (2012), showed degeneracies of quantum black holes are Fourier coefficients of mock modular forms; the "completion" restores the modular symmetry expected from AdS₃/CFT₂ holography, with a holomorphic anomaly reflecting non-compactness. This extends the Hardy–Ramanujan–Rademacher expansion to black-hole microstate counting.
  - **Moonshine:** Eguchi, Ooguri, Tachikawa (2010/2011), "Notes on the K3 Surface and the Mathieu group M₂₄," observed the K3 elliptic genus decomposes with Mathieu-group M₂₄ dimensions (Mathieu moonshine). Cheng, Duncan, and Harvey, "Umbral Moonshine" (2014), generalized this to six groups tied to the divisors of 12 and the Niemeier lattices, with the relevant mock modular forms drawn from the Dabholkar–Murthy–Zagier family.

### 5. THE TAU FUNCTION AND MODULAR FORMS

Ramanujan (1916), "On certain arithmetical functions," studied τ(n) via Δ(z) = q∏(1−qⁿ)²⁴ = Σ τ(n)qⁿ = η(z)²⁴, and conjectured:
1. τ(mn) = τ(m)τ(n) for gcd(m,n)=1 (multiplicativity);
2. τ(p^(r+1)) = τ(p)τ(p^r) − p¹¹τ(p^(r−1));
3. |τ(p)| ≤ 2p^(11/2) (the growth bound).

- **(b) Proof status:** Mordell (1917), "On Mr. Ramanujan's empirical expansions of modular functions," proved (1) and (2) using what became Hecke operators. The third — the **Ramanujan conjecture** — was proved by Pierre Deligne in 1974 as a consequence of his proof of the Weil conjectures (applied to a Kuga–Sato variety); this was part of the work for which he won the Fields Medal (1978).
- **(c) Compression:** multiplicativity means the whole doubly-infinite table of τ(n) is generated by its values at primes plus one recursion — an Euler product L(s,Δ) = ∏(1 − τ(p)p^(−s) + p^(11−2s))^(−1). The growth bound is a sharp envelope on an otherwise wild arithmetic function.
- **(d) The steal / Langlands:** the growth bound generalizes to the **Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture** — that the Satake parameters of automorphic representations are tempered — a central open problem across the Langlands program (still open in general, e.g. for Maass forms it is the Selberg conjecture). Tau congruences (e.g. mod 691) and their explanation via Galois representations (Serre, Swinnerton-Dyer; Deligne–Serre) foreshadowed the modularity machinery later central to Fermat's Last Theorem. Lehmer's conjecture (τ(n) ≠ 0 for all n) remains open.

### 6. RAMANUJAN GRAPHS — the CS steal

Lubotzky, Phillips, and Sarnak (1988), "Ramanujan graphs" (*Combinatorica* 8:261–277), constructed explicit k-regular Cayley graphs whose nontrivial eigenvalues satisfy |λ| ≤ 2√(k−1).

- **(a) What it states:** a Ramanujan graph is a k-regular graph whose non-trivial adjacency eigenvalues are bounded by 2√(k−1) — the spectrally optimal expanders. As LPS state, "this property is optimal and leads to the best known explicit expander graphs."
- **(b) Why "Ramanujan":** the eigenvalue bound follows from the Ramanujan(–Petersson) conjecture (proved by Deligne/Eichler for the relevant modular forms) — hence the name. The bound is optimal by the Alon–Boppana theorem, which says any family of k-regular graphs has second eigenvalue ≥ 2√(k−1) − o(1). Margulis (1988) independently gave explicit constructions.
- **(c) Compression:** a Ramanujan graph packs maximal connectivity/mixing into minimal degree — "the best expanders" — squeezing the spectral gap to its theoretical limit.
- **(d) The steal / applications:** expanders are foundational in CS — error-correcting codes, derandomization (pseudorandomness), network robustness, sorting networks, and more. Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava (2013–2015), "Interlacing Families I: Bipartite Ramanujan Graphs of All Degrees" (*Annals of Mathematics* 182:307–325), used the method of interlacing polynomials and a signing argument to prove bipartite Ramanujan graphs exist for *every* degree (not just prime-power ± 1); the same interlacing technique solved the Kadison–Singer problem.

### 7. ROGERS–RAMANUJAN IDENTITIES

The two identities:
Σ q^(n²)/(q;q)_n = 1/∏(1−q^(5m+1))(1−q^(5m+4)) and Σ q^(n²+n)/(q;q)_n = 1/∏(1−q^(5m+2))(1−q^(5m+3)).

- **(a) Combinatorial meaning:** the first says the number of partitions of n into parts differing by at least 2 equals the number into parts ≡ 1 or 4 (mod 5); the second is the analogue for parts ≡ 2 or 3 (mod 5).
- **(b) History:** first proved by L.J. Rogers (1894, *Proc. London Math. Soc.*), forgotten, then rediscovered by Ramanujan around 1917 (Ramanujan found them in his notebook and, unable to prove them, later found Rogers' paper). The famous count "about 130" refers to Slater's list of 130 identities of Rogers–Ramanujan *type* (L.J. Slater, *Proc. London Math. Soc.* (2) 54 (1952), 147–167), not 130 distinct proofs of the two identities themselves. The related continued fraction R(q) = q^(1/5) G(q)/H(q) has values expressible as nested radicals at imaginary-quadratic points (via Shimura reciprocity).
- **(c) Compression:** each identity equates an entire q-series (a sum over all partitions of a restricted type) to a compact infinite product — an exact "sum side = product side" encoding of infinitely many partition-counting facts in a three-symbol closed form.
- **(d) The steal:**
  - **Statistical mechanics:** Rodney Baxter's 1980 solution of the hard-hexagon model (*J. Phys. A* 13:L61) — a 2D lattice gas of particles on a triangular lattice with a nearest-neighbor exclusion — required Rogers–Ramanujan-type identities to express its one-dimensional partition functions as infinite products. George Andrews (PNAS 1981) proved the six/eight relevant identities. This was the entrance of these identities into physics and launched the field of exactly solved models.
  - **Lie theory / CFT:** Lepowsky and Wilson (1981–1985, "The structure of standard modules") gave a Lie-theoretic interpretation and proof via level-3 standard modules of the affine Lie algebra A₁⁽¹⁾ (Heisenberg subalgebra, Z-operators, principally specialized Weyl–Kac character formula) — a founding result of vertex operator algebra theory. The identities relate to the (2,5) Virasoro minimal model / Lee–Yang edge singularity in conformal field theory.

### 8. RAMANUJAN SUMMATION AND DIVERGENT SERIES

The notorious "1 + 2 + 3 + ⋯ = −1/12," which Ramanujan communicated to Hardy in his first letter (16 January 1913).

- **(a) Rigorous meaning:** this is *not* an ordinary sum. It is the value assigned by Ramanujan summation / zeta-function regularization — the analytic continuation of ζ(s) = Σ n^(−s) to s = −1, where ζ(−1) = −1/12. Hardy urged "great caution"; no linear, stable summation method sums the series to a finite value.
- **(c) Compression:** it extracts a finite "principal part" from a divergent object in a consistent, calibrated way (agreeing with ordinary sums where those converge).
- **(d) The steal in physics:**
  - **Bosonic string theory:** the zero-point energy of a quantum string sums the harmonics as −ħω(D−2)/24; consistency with Lorentz invariance (via the Goddard–Thorn "no-ghost" theorem) forces (D−2)/24 = 1, i.e. **D = 26** — the critical dimension of bosonic string theory falls straight out of ζ(−1) = −1/12.
  - **Casimir effect:** the regularized sum yields the attractive force between two uncharged conducting plates for a scalar field; the negative sign of −1/12 reflects attraction.
  - **QFT:** zeta-function regularization (Hawking, 1977, "Zeta function regularization of path integrals in curved spacetime") is a standard tool for one-loop effective actions.

### 9. OTHER USABLE LATEXIUM

- **Ramanujan's Master Theorem** (in his notebooks; rigor supplied by Hardy via Mellin inversion and Cauchy's residue theorem): if f(x) = Σ φ(k)(−x)ᵏ/k!, then ∫₀^∞ x^(s−1) f(x) dx = Γ(s) φ(−s). **Compression:** it reads a Mellin transform straight off the Taylor coefficients (analytically continued). **The steal:** it is the engine of the **method of brackets** for evaluating **Feynman integrals** in high-energy physics (Gonzalez, Moll, Straub, and others) and multivariate Mellin–Barnes integrals; recent work extends it to cosmological amplitudes and to meromorphic functions.
- **Highly composite numbers:** "Highly Composite Numbers," *Proc. London Math. Soc.* (2) 14 (1915), pp. 347–409 — about 62 pages, his longest paper, and the basis of his 1916 Cambridge research degree. Ramanujan defined a highly composite number as one with more divisors than any smaller integer. The LMS, in financial difficulty, asked him to shorten it, so the published version was incomplete; the remainder was published by Nicolas and Robin (*Ramanujan Journal* 1, 1997). Erdős (with Alaoglu, 1944) extended the theory. **Echo in complexity theory:** these numbers are extremal objects for the divisor function, relevant to worst-case analyses and to the Riemann-hypothesis-equivalent Robin's inequality.
- **Ramanujan primes:** from his two-page "A proof of Bertrand's postulate," *J. Indian Math. Soc.* 11 (1919), 181–182. The nth Ramanujan prime R_n is the smallest integer such that π(x) − π(x/2) ≥ n for all x ≥ R_n; first values 2, 11, 17, 29, 41 (R₁ = 2 is Bertrand's postulate). The term was coined by Jonathan Sondow (*Amer. Math. Monthly* 116, 2009), who showed R_n ~ p_{2n}.
- **Landau–Ramanujan constant:** K ≈ 0.7642236535… — the density constant in N(x) ~ K·x/√(ln x) for integers ≤ x that are sums of two squares. Found independently by Landau (1908, rigorous) and Ramanujan (stated in his first letter to Hardy, 1913); see Moree–Cazaran (*Expo. Math.* 17, 1999).
- **Nested radicals:** √(1 + 2√(1 + 3√(1 + 4√(⋯)))) = 3, posed by Ramanujan as Question 289, *J. Indian Math. Soc.* (1911); he supplied the answer after six months with no submitted solutions. **Compression:** an infinite self-similar radical collapses to a single integer.
- **Ellipse perimeter:** Ramanujan's approximation P ≈ π(a+b)[1 + 3λ²/(10 + √(4−3λ²))], λ = (a−b)/(a+b), is astonishingly accurate (error bounded, shrinking with eccentricity) and is still used in engineering; a rigorous best-constant error analysis was given by Villarino, and its continued-fraction origin has been reconstructed.
- **1729 and the K3 surface:** Hardy's taxicab anecdote (1729 = 1³+12³ = 9³+10³, the smallest number so expressible in two ways) is documented in Hardy's *Ramanujan* lectures; but Ono and Trebat-Leder, "The 1729 K3 Surface" (*Research in Number Theory*, 2015; arXiv 1510.00735), showed Ramanujan had written the property down before England and had in fact been studying Euler's equation a³+b³=c³+d³, effectively discovering a K3 surface of Picard number 18 (giving infinitely many cubic twists of rank ≥ 2) decades before K3 surfaces were named by Weil.
- **e^(π√163) and Heegner numbers:** e^(π√163) = 262537412640768743.99999999999925… ≈ 640320³ + 744 — an "almost integer" because 163 is the largest Heegner number (class number 1 for ℚ(√−163), so the j-invariant is an algebraic integer). *Caveat:* the near-integer was first noted by Hermite (1859); the name "Ramanujan's constant" derives from a Martin Gardner April Fool's hoax in *Scientific American* (April 1975) — Ramanujan did not state this specific near-identity, though he gave many other spectacular almost-integers and the underlying class-invariant theory.
- **Ramanujan–Nagell equation:** x² = 2ⁿ − 7, conjectured by Ramanujan (1913) to have exactly the solutions n = 3, 4, 5, 7, 15 (x = 1, 3, 5, 11, 181); proved by Trygve Nagell (1948). It governs the non-existence of certain perfect binary codes.
- **Class invariants g_n and G_n:** the Ramanujan class invariants (Weber-type) underlie the singular moduli that make the π series and Rogers–Ramanujan continued-fraction evaluations work; g₅₈ is the invariant behind the classic 1/π series.

### 10. THE MACHINE-VERIFICATION ANGLE

- **The Ramanujan Machine** (Technion, led by Ido Kaminer; Raayoni et al., *Nature* 590 (2021), 67–73; arXiv 1907.00205, 2019): algorithms (a meet-in-the-middle variant and a gradient-descent method tuned to continued fractions) that automatically *generate conjectured* continued-fraction formulas for constants like π, e, Catalan's constant, and ζ-values by numerical pattern-matching. Some conjectures were proved (often easily, in retrospect); others remain open. Critics (Frank Calegari) argued many outputs are special cases of the Gauss continued fraction (known since 1813); George Andrews called the naming "slightly outrageous" but conceded that improving the Catalan constant's irrationality exponent shows real contributions. A later theoretical outgrowth is the "conservative matrix field" unifying thousands of such formulas.
- **Wilf–Zeilberger theory / creative telescoping:** the industrialization of hypergeometric identity proving — the WZ method mechanically certifies (and discovers) such identities. Ekhad and Zeilberger produced a WZ proof of a Ramanujan 1/π formula; this is the closest thing to a general "compiler" for the hypergeometric fragment of Ramanujan's corpus.
- **Experimental mathematics:** the Borwein–Bailey program (*Mathematics by Experiment*, *Pi and the AGM*) used high-precision computation and integer-relation algorithms (PSLQ) to discover and then prove Ramanujan-type identities — the methodological bridge from Ramanujan's intuition to reproducible algorithmic discovery.
- **Formal verification:** progress on formalizing Ramanujan-specific results in Lean/Coq is limited compared to the analytic machinery around it; the ecosystem's formalized modular-forms and partition infrastructure is growing but the specific Ramanujan identities are, for the most part, not yet fully machine-checked — an open opportunity for a grimoire project.

### 11. THE HUMAN STORY, PROPERLY SOURCED

- **Birth/death:** born 22 December 1887, Erode; died 26 April 1920, near Madras, aged 32 (cause now attributed by D.A.B. Young, 1994, to hepatic amoebiasis).
- **The 1913 letter and Hardy's reaction:** Ramanujan's letters of 16 January and 27 February 1913 are "two of the most famous letters in the history of mathematics" (Berndt & Rankin, *Ramanujan: Letters and Commentary*, AMS 1995). Hardy's verdict (via Kanigel's *The Man Who Knew Infinity*): the formulas "must be true because, if they were not true, no one would have the imagination to invent them" — the theorems "defeated me completely; I had never seen anything in the least like them before... they could only be written down by a mathematician of the highest class." Wording varies slightly across sources; the substance traces to Hardy's Ramanujan lectures.
- **Hardy's rating scale:** on a 0–100 scale of natural mathematical talent, Hardy gave himself 25, Littlewood 30, Hilbert 80, and Ramanujan 100 (relayed by Paul Erdős; reported by Berndt and in Kanigel).
- **The Namagiri dreams (legend vs. documented):** Ramanujan attributed insights to the goddess Namagiri, and said an equation "has no meaning unless it represents a thought of God." Documented: his mother's dream sanctioning his travel to England; Ramanujan's own statements crediting Namagiri. The vivid image of equations "written on his tongue" / unrolling on scrolls is reported biographically (Kanigel and others) but is best treated as Ramanujan's self-description rather than externally verified fact — legend embroidered over time.
- **The slate-and-elbow method:** Ramanujan worked on a slate, erasing with his elbow to save paper, transferring only final results to his notebooks — hence the missing proofs. (Reported in Kanigel and the standard biographies.)
- **The 1729 story:** as Hardy told it (in his *Ramanujan* lectures), he arrived in taxicab 1729, called it dull, and Ramanujan instantly replied it is "the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways." The core is documented; embellishments ("burst into the room") are later accretions, and Ramanujan had recorded the property earlier (Ono–Trebat-Leder).
- **Honors:** elected Fellow of the Royal Society in 1918 — "distinguished as a pure mathematician particularly for his investigations in elliptic functions and the theory of numbers." He was the second Indian FRS (the first was Ardaseer Cursetjee, 1841) but the first Indian mathematician so elected. He was elected a Fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge on 13 October 1918 — the first Indian to be so — a Prize Fellowship of £250/year for six years.
- **Illness, return, death:** he fell seriously ill in England (tuberculosis was long suspected; later reanalysis favors hepatic amoebiasis), returned to India in 1919, and died 26 April 1920 at 32.
- **Janaki:** his wife S. Janaki Ammal (married 14 July 1909) survived him by about 74 years, dying 13 April 1994 aged 94. She received a University of Madras pension (initially ~Rs. 20/month, rising to ~Rs. 500/month), lived as a seamstress in Triplicane, Chennai, and adopted a foster son.
- **Berndt's decades of verification:** Bruce Berndt (University of Illinois) devoted his research from May 1977 to the notebooks, enlisting George Andrews (Penn State) and others, producing the five-volume *Ramanujan's Notebooks* (1985–1998) and the five Andrews–Berndt *Ramanujan's Lost Notebook* volumes (2005–2018).

## Recommendations

For a VR "grimoire" that treats identities as executable content on Goldberg-polyhedron gameplay, stage the build by *algorithmic usability*:

1. **Ship first the constructively computable "compressions" (highest usability):**
   - **π series (Chudnovsky/Ramanujan) + binary splitting** and the **AGM/Gauss–Legendre** iteration — these are literally algorithms with a crisp "digits-per-term" (or per-iteration) metric; ideal for a "compute-a-constant" mechanic. Benchmark: ~14.18 digits/term (Chudnovsky), ~8 (Ramanujan-58), ~74 and ~153 (Berndt–Chan and the 2024 higher-order series), doubling per step (AGM).
   - **Rademacher's exact p(n) series** — a decompression of an infinite combinatorial object into a convergent sum; perfect for a "reconstruct the partition count" puzzle.
   - **Rogers–Ramanujan identities** — "sum side ↔ product side" is a natural two-panel matching mechanic, and it maps cleanly to partitions (parts differing by ≥2 vs. residues mod 5).
2. **Build second the "verifier/generator" layer:** integrate **Wilf–Zeilberger creative telescoping** as an in-game "proof certificate" engine for hypergeometric identities, and mimic the **Ramanujan Machine** (meet-in-the-middle + gradient descent on continued fractions) as a "conjecture-forge" mode. These give you procedurally generated, checkable content.
3. **Use as "lore/structure" (not directly executable) the deep-theory items:** mock theta functions ↔ black holes/moonshine, tau ↔ Langlands, Ramanujan graphs ↔ expanders. Ramanujan graphs specifically are a natural fit for network/level-connectivity design and can be *generated* (LPS or Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava constructions) — a rare deep-theory item that is also algorithmically constructive.
4. **Ship the failure ledger as a game mechanic:** include Ramanujan's *wrong* prime-counting formulas explicitly (the "zeta zeros off the line" bug) as cautionary/boss content — it teaches why decompression can fail and rewards accuracy.

**Thresholds that change the plan:** if you need *formally verified* content (Lean/Coq), restrict v1 to the π series, partitions, and Rogers–Ramanujan (best-supported by existing machine-checkable proofs and WZ certificates), and defer mock-modular/moonshine content to "lore" until formalization matures. If performance is the constraint, prefer AGM-type iterations (fewer, heavier steps) over series (many light steps) or vice-versa depending on your arithmetic backend.

## Caveats

- **No exact "proven/rediscovered/wrong" percentages exist.** The literature gives Berndt's count of 3,254 examined results and qualitative statements (majority true and often new; a minority prior rediscoveries; a small number false). Treat any precise fraction as unsupported.
- **"−1/12," "e^(π√163) = Ramanujan's constant," and the 1729 anecdote are frequently mis-told.** −1/12 is a regularized value, not a sum; the e^(π√163) near-integer was Hermite's (1859) and the "Ramanujan" name comes from a 1975 Gardner hoax; the 1729 story's core is real but embellished.
- **Some named results are joint or mis-attributed in popular sources.** Ramanujan was the *second* Indian FRS, not the first; the FRS election date appears variously as 28 February or May 1918 (cite "1918"); the Highly Composite Numbers paper is ~62 pages (pp. 347–409), not ~50; and the deathbed letter is dated 12 January 1920 (one outlier source says 20 January). The "130" for Rogers–Ramanujan refers to Slater's list of related identities, not proofs of the two.
- **Open problems remain in the surrounding theory even though the corpus is "closed":** the general Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture (Langlands), Lehmer's conjecture (τ(n) ≠ 0), and the proof status of various Ramanujan-Machine continued-fraction conjectures are all still active.
- **Formal-verification coverage is uneven.** Analytic and modular-forms infrastructure is expanding in proof assistants, but most *specific* Ramanujan identities are not yet fully formalized; claims to the contrary should be checked against current Lean/Coq mathlib status before being built into a product.
- **The "orders" of mock theta functions (3, 5, 7) and the count "17" reflect Ramanujan's own grouping;** later work identified further orders (6, 8, 10). Use "17 examples in orders 3, 5, 7" as Ramanujan's original list, not an exhaustive modern taxonomy.
- **π-digit records move fast:** the figures above are current to the November 2025 record of 314 trillion digits; expect further advances.
