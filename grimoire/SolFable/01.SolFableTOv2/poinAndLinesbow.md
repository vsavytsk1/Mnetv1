# The Grimoire of the Titans: Foundational Code Blocks and Algorithms That Built the Interconnected World

## TL;DR
- The internet is, at bottom, two mathematical disciplines stepped into silicon: **graph theory** (Euler, 1736 — vertices and edges that "don't quite exist") and **information theory** (Shannon, 1948 — everything reduced to 1s and 0s). Nearly every foundational algorithm below is one of those two ideas made executable to defeat a specific, often bizarre, failure mode.
- This survey documents roughly 70 algorithms and named failure modes across nine domains, each attributed to the individual "titans" who created it, with the counterintuitive problem it solved, the algorithmic core, and the original paper and date.
- The recurring human pattern is striking: many of these were invented fast, alone, and against skepticism — Dijkstra's shortest path in 20 minutes at a café, Huffman's optimal code the morning he gave up studying for the exam, Perlman's spanning tree in about a week (with a poem), and Lamport's Paxos rejected as insignificant for nearly a decade.

## Key Findings

1. **The two roots are literally datable.** Graph theory begins with Euler's 1736 solution to the Seven Bridges of Königsberg, which abstracted a city into points (vertices) and connections (edges) that have no physical existence — the founding move of "networks." Information theory begins with Shannon's 1948 "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," which proved that any message could be reduced to bits and transmitted below a hard capacity limit with arbitrarily low error. Everything else is downstream.

2. **The hardest problems were failures nobody predicted.** The 1986 NSFNET congestion collapse (throughput crashing from 32 kbit/s to 40 bit/s), the count-to-infinity routing loop, the AS 7007 BGP black hole, and the Nagle/delayed-ACK deadlock were all emergent, counterintuitive failures of systems that "should" have worked. The fixes — Jacobson's congestion control, split horizon/poison reverse, BGP filtering, TCP_NODELAY — are the crown jewels of the field.

3. **Attribution matters and is often mangled.** Public-key cryptography should arguably be "Diffie–Hellman–Merkle." LDPC codes sat ignored for 30 years before rediscovery. Radia Perlman dislikes "Mother of the Internet." This report gives credit precisely, with dates and citations.

### PART 1 — GRAPH THEORY FOUNDATIONS: points and lines that don't quite exist

**Euler and the Seven Bridges of Königsberg (1736).** Leonhard Euler proved that no walk could cross all seven bridges of Königsberg exactly once. His decisive abstraction was to throw away the geography and represent land masses as vertices and bridges as edges — objects with no physical extent. The result: such a walk (an Eulerian path) exists only if exactly zero or two vertices have odd degree. Paper: *Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis* (1736). This is the origin of "points and lines that don't quite exist" and the founding document of graph theory and topology.

**Kirchhoff's Matrix–Tree Theorem (1847).** Gustav Kirchhoff, working on electrical circuits, proved that the number of spanning trees of a graph equals any cofactor of its Laplacian matrix (degree matrix minus adjacency matrix). This is the first deep bridge between a *combinatorial* count (spanning trees) and *linear algebra* (a determinant), and the seed of spectral graph theory.

**Spectral graph theory & expander graphs.** The eigenvalues of the adjacency/Laplacian matrix encode connectivity, mixing, and expansion. **Expander graphs** — sparse graphs that are nonetheless extremely well-connected (large spectral gap) — are the counterintuitive stars: they behave like random graphs but can be explicitly constructed, and they underpin error-correcting codes, pseudorandomness, and network robustness.

**Erdős–Rényi random graphs (1959–1960).** Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi, "On the Evolution of Random Graphs" (1960). Take n vertices, add each possible edge independently with probability p. Their shocking discovery was the **phase transition**: as p crosses 1/n, a giant connected component appears almost instantaneously. Connectivity is not gradual — it snaps into existence at a threshold.

**Watts–Strogatz small-world networks (1998).** Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz, "Collective dynamics of 'small-world' networks," *Nature* 393 (1998). Rewiring a few edges of a regular lattice at random collapses the average path length to near-random levels while preserving high clustering — the mathematics behind "six degrees of separation."

**Barabási–Albert scale-free networks (1999).** Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert, "Emergence of scaling in random networks," *Science* 286 (1999). **Preferential attachment** ("the rich get richer") produces degree distributions following a power law — explaining why real networks (the web, citations) have a few enormous hubs rather than the bell curve Erdős–Rényi predicts.

**PageRank as a graph eigenvector problem (1998).** Sergey Brin and Lawrence Page, "The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine," *Computer Networks and ISDN Systems* 30 (1998), and the companion Stanford tech report "The PageRank Citation Ranking: Bringing Order to the Web" (Page, Brin, Motwani, Winograd). The problem: rank web pages by importance when "importance" is circular (important pages are those linked by important pages). The solution: model a **random surfer** walking the web graph; a page's rank is its stationary probability — the dominant eigenvector of the (damped) link matrix. A damping factor (~0.85) models the surfer occasionally teleporting to a random page, guaranteeing the Markov chain is ergodic and the eigenvector unique. Exact mathematics (Markov chains + Perron–Frobenius) stepped into a multi-billion-dollar company.

**Kleinberg's HITS (1999).** Jon Kleinberg, "Authoritative Sources in a Hyperlinked Environment" (J. ACM, 1999). Independently of PageRank, HITS assigns each page two scores — **hub** (points to good authorities) and **authority** (pointed to by good hubs) — computed as the principal eigenvectors of A·Aᵀ and Aᵀ·A. Same underlying idea: importance is an eigenvector.

**Paul Baran's redundancy analysis (1964).** Before any of this, Paul Baran at RAND did the founding graph-theoretic analysis of network *survivability*. In "On Distributed Communications" (eleven volumes, 1964), he classified networks as centralized, decentralized, and distributed, and showed by simulation that a distributed mesh with a **"redundancy level" of about 3** (roughly three times the minimum number of links needed to connect all nodes) could survive massive node loss — e.g., a nuclear first strike. His conclusion that analog signals degrade over many hops forced the move to **digital** and to what we now call packet switching (he called it "distributed adaptive message block switching"). Donald Davies at the UK National Physical Laboratory independently invented the same thing and gave it the winning name "packet switching," down to a nearly identical 1024-bit packet size.

### PART 2 — ROUTING: getting a packet through a graph that keeps changing

**Dijkstra's shortest path (conceived 1956, published 1959).** Edsger W. Dijkstra, "A note on two problems in connexion with graphs," *Numerische Mathematik* 1 (1959), pp. 269–271. **The story:** Dijkstra conceived it in about 20 minutes in 1956, sitting at a café terrace in Amsterdam with his fiancée Maria (Ria) Debets, tired after shopping, with no pencil and paper. In his own words: *"What's the shortest way to travel from Rotterdam to Groningen? … I designed it in about 20 minutes … Without pencil and paper you are almost forced to avoid all avoidable complexities."* He first used it to show off the ARMAC computer, and later called it "one of the cornerstones of my fame." **Core:** maintain tentative distances from a source; repeatedly extract the unvisited vertex of minimum distance, finalize it, and relax its outgoing edges. Requires non-negative edge weights; runs in O((V+E) log V) with a binary heap.

**Bellman–Ford (1958).** Richard Bellman and Lester Ford Jr. Handles **negative edge weights** and is inherently distributed: each node repeatedly updates its distance estimate from its neighbors' estimates (relaxation), converging in V−1 rounds. This is the direct ancestor of **distance-vector routing** — and, as we'll see, of the count-to-infinity disease.

**Ford–Fulkerson max-flow (1956).** Lester Ford and Delbert Fulkerson. Computes maximum flow through a capacitated network by repeatedly finding augmenting paths in the residual graph. The max-flow min-cut theorem — the maximum flow equals the minimum cut capacity — is one of the most beautiful duality results in combinatorics.

**Floyd–Warshall (1962).** Robert Floyd (building on Stephen Warshall). All-pairs shortest paths via a triple loop that asks, for every pair (i,j), whether routing through intermediate k is shorter. O(V³), elegant, and the standard for dense graphs.

**A\* search (1968).** Peter Hart, Nils Nilsson, Bertram Raphael (SRI), "A Formal Basis for the Heuristic Determination of Minimum Cost Paths." Dijkstra plus an **admissible heuristic** h(n) that never overestimates remaining cost, directing search toward the goal. Foundational for maps, games, and robotics.

**The original ARPANET routing algorithm and its failure.** The early ARPANET used a distance-vector scheme that suffered slow convergence and loops; it was replaced in 1979 by a **link-state** algorithm (John McQuillan et al.), in which every router floods the whole network with the state of its own links and independently computes shortest paths via Dijkstra. Link-state converges faster and avoids count-to-infinity, at the cost of more flooding.

**The COUNT-TO-INFINITY problem (the heart-of-darkness of distance-vector).** In distance-vector routing (Bellman–Ford), when a link fails, routers can pass increasingly wrong distance estimates back and forth — each believing the other still has a route — incrementing the hop count one at a time toward infinity while a loop persists. **Fixes:** *Split horizon* (never advertise a route back to the neighbor you learned it from); *poison reverse* (advertise it back but with metric = infinity, "bad news travels fast"); *route poisoning* and *hold-down timers*. RIP defines "infinity" as 16 to bound the damage. This is the canonical example of a distributed algorithm being *locally* correct but *globally* pathological.

**Link-state vs distance-vector.** Distance-vector: tell your neighbors about the whole world (cheap messages, slow/loopy convergence). Link-state: tell the whole world about your neighbors (expensive flooding, fast/loop-free convergence). BGP is a third kind — **path-vector** — which carries the entire AS-path so loops are detected by inspection.

**BGP path-vector routing.** The Border Gateway Protocol glues the autonomous systems of the internet together. Each route advertisement carries the full list of ASes it has traversed; a router rejects any path already containing its own AS number (loop prevention). BGP is policy-driven, not shortest-path — and its trust-by-default design is the source of some of the internet's worst failures (Part 9).

**Radia Perlman's Spanning Tree Protocol (1985).** Radia Perlman, "An Algorithm for Distributed Computation of a Spanning Tree in an Extended LAN," *SIGCOMM '85*, pp. 44–53 (later IEEE 802.1D). **The problem:** Ethernet bridges forward broadcasts out every port; any loop in the topology causes packets to circulate and multiply forever — a **broadcast storm** that melts the network. Redundant links (needed for reliability) are exactly what kill you. **The solution:** bridges elect a root (lowest ID), each bridge computes its least-cost path to the root, and all other redundant links are *blocked* — leaving a loop-free spanning tree that still spans every LAN. Her manager had handed her the problem as "probably unsolvable"; she solved it in about a week and wrote the specification's abstract as a **poem, "Algorhyme"**:

> *I think that I shall never see / A graph more lovely than a tree. / A tree whose crucial property / Is loop-free connectivity … / First, the root must be selected. / By ID, it is elected. / Least-cost paths from root are traced. / In the tree, these paths are placed. / A mesh is made by folks like me, / Then bridges find a spanning tree.*

It is likely the only software patent on record that contains a poem. Perlman rejects the "Mother of the Internet" label, insisting no one person invented it.

**Longest-prefix-match, Patricia tries, radix trees.** IP forwarding must find the *most specific* (longest) matching prefix for a destination address among hundreds of thousands of routes. The **Patricia trie** (Practical Algorithm To Retrieve Information Coded In Alphanumeric; Donald Morrison, 1968) and radix trees compress the bit-by-bit search into a path-compressed tree, making longest-prefix-match feasible at line rate. This is graph theory (a trie is a tree) solving a lookup problem.

### PART 3 — CONGESTION CONTROL: the tragedy of the commons in packets

**The 1986 NSFNET congestion collapse.** In October 1986 the throughput between Lawrence Berkeley Lab and UC Berkeley collapsed by a factor of a thousand. As Van Jacobson and Michael Karels recount in "Congestion Avoidance and Control" (SIGCOMM '88): *"the data throughput from LBL to UC Berkeley (sites separated by 400 yards and three IMP hops) dropped from 32 Kbps to 40 bps … this sudden factor-of-thousand drop in bandwidth."* The cause: when packets were dropped, naive TCP retransmitted them, adding *more* load, dropping *more* packets — a positive feedback death spiral. John Nagle had warned of "congestive collapse" as possible in 1984 (RFC 896).

**Van Jacobson and Michael Karels, "Congestion Avoidance and Control" (1988).** *SIGCOMM '88*. Fascinated by that thousand-fold drop, Jacobson (LBL) and Karels (UC Berkeley) diagnosed 4.3BSD TCP and added seven algorithms. The organizing principle was **"conservation of packets"**: don't inject a new packet until an old one leaves (an ACK returns). Their patch was only a few lines of BSD code and the internet has not had a systemic congestion collapse since.

- **Slow start:** start with a tiny congestion window (cwnd) and double it every RTT (exponential growth) until a threshold, probing capacity gently rather than blasting.
- **AIMD (Additive Increase, Multiplicative Decrease):** in steady state, increase cwnd by ~1 segment per RTT (additive), but on packet loss *halve* it (multiplicative). AIMD provably converges to a fair, efficient equilibrium — the mathematical reason the shared internet is stable.
- **Fast retransmit / fast recovery:** three duplicate ACKs signal a lost packet; retransmit immediately without waiting for a timeout.

**Karn's algorithm (1987).** Phil Karn and Craig Partridge. **The problem:** when you retransmit a packet, and an ACK arrives, you can't tell whether it acknowledges the original or the retransmission — so you can't measure RTT reliably (retransmission ambiguity). **The fix:** don't update the RTT estimate from retransmitted segments at all, and use exponential timer backoff for repeated losses.

**Nagle's algorithm (1984).** John Nagle, RFC 896. **The problem** ("small-packet problem" / "tinygrams"): a Telnet session sending one keystroke generates a 41-byte packet for 1 byte of data — 4000% overhead. **The fix:** if there is unacknowledged data outstanding, buffer small writes until a full segment can be sent or the ACK arrives.

**The pathological Nagle/delayed-ACK interaction.** Delayed ACK (RFC 813, ~1982, developed independently at Berkeley) holds back an ACK up to ~200–500 ms hoping to piggyback it on a reply. Combined with Nagle, you get a deadlock: the sender won't send more until it gets an ACK; the receiver won't ACK until it gets more (or the timer fires). Applications doing write-write-read stall for up to 500 ms. Nagle himself calls the combination "awful" and blames the fixed-timer delayed ACK; the standard cure is TCP_NODELAY. This is the archetypal case of two individually reasonable features interacting to produce a disaster.

**Silly Window Syndrome (David Clark, RFC 813).** When a slow receiver advertises tiny amounts of newly freed buffer space, the sender dribbles out tiny segments, and the connection degenerates into maximal overhead. Fixes on both sender (Nagle) and receiver (don't advertise small windows) sides.

**RED — Random Early Detection (1993).** Sally Floyd and Van Jacobson, "Random Early Detection Gateways for Congestion Avoidance," IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking. **The problem:** tail-drop queues drop packets only when full, causing *global synchronization* (below) and large latencies. **The fix:** as the average queue length grows, the router drops (or marks) packets with increasing *probability* before the queue is full — signaling congestion early and de-synchronizing the senders.

**TCP global synchronization.** With tail-drop, all flows overflow the queue at the same instant, all halve their windows simultaneously, all ramp back up in lockstep, and all overflow again — the link oscillates between congested and idle, wasting capacity. RED's randomized early drop breaks this lockstep.

**CUBIC (2008).** Sangtae Ha, Injong Rhee, Lisong Xu. The default Linux congestion control. Replaces AIMD's linear growth with a **cubic function** of time since the last loss, making it efficient on high-bandwidth, high-latency ("long fat") networks while remaining fair.

**BBR (2016).** Neal Cardwell, Yuchung Cheng, C. Stephen Gunn, Soheil Hassas Yeganeh, Van Jacobson (Google). **The insight:** loss-based control mistakes buffering for congestion. BBR instead **models the bottleneck** — continuously estimating bottleneck bandwidth and round-trip propagation delay — and paces packets to operate at the optimal point (Kleinrock's), keeping buffers nearly empty. Fixes bufferbloat-induced latency.

**Bufferbloat and CoDel (2012).** Kathleen Nichols and Van Jacobson, "Controlling Queue Delay." **The problem:** cheap, oversized buffers everywhere accumulate huge standing queues, so latency balloons to seconds even without loss ("bufferbloat"). **CoDel (Controlled Delay):** track the *minimum* queueing delay over a window; if packets are consistently spending too long in the queue (>5 ms target), start dropping — a parameterless, self-tuning AQM.

### PART 4 — ERROR CORRECTION AND INFORMATION THEORY: beating noise

**Shannon's channel capacity (1948).** Claude Shannon, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," *Bell System Technical Journal*, 1948. The founding document of the information age. It established the **bit** as the unit of information, entropy H = −Σ p log p as its measure, and the **noisy-channel coding theorem**: every channel has a capacity C, and for any rate below C there exist codes achieving arbitrarily small error probability — but above C, reliable communication is impossible. Shannon proved good codes *exist* without saying how to build them; the next 60 years were spent chasing his limit.

**Hamming codes (1950).** Richard Hamming, "Error Detecting and Error Correcting Codes," *Bell System Technical Journal* 29 (April 1950). **The story:** Hamming had computer access only on weekends; twice in a row the relay machine hit an error on Friday night and dumped his job, wasting the whole weekend. His famous reaction, from a taped interview: *"Damn it, if the machine can detect an error, why can't it locate the position of the error and correct it?"* **The core:** place parity bits at power-of-two positions; each checks a specific subset of bit positions so that the pattern of failed checks (the "syndrome") is the binary *address* of the flipped bit. Hamming(7,4) encodes 4 data bits in 7, corrects any single-bit error, detects any double. Minimum distance 3.

**CRC — Cyclic Redundancy Check (1961).** W. Wesley Peterson. Treats the message as a polynomial over GF(2) and appends the remainder after division by a fixed generator polynomial. Cheap in hardware (shift registers), excellent at detecting burst errors — used in Ethernet, ZIP, and nearly every link layer.

**Reed–Solomon codes (1960).** Irving Reed and Gustave Solomon, "Polynomial Codes over Certain Finite Fields," J. SIAM. **The core:** treat data as coefficients of a polynomial over a finite field and transmit its values at many points; because any k points determine a degree-(k−1) polynomial, you can lose or corrupt many symbols and still recover it. The workhorse of CDs, DVDs, QR codes, DSL, and deep-space communication (Voyager).

**Viterbi algorithm (1967).** Andrew Viterbi. **The core:** optimal (maximum-likelihood) decoding of convolutional codes via dynamic programming over a trellis — it finds the single most likely transmitted sequence in linear time rather than searching exponentially many. Ubiquitous in mobile phones (GSM, CDMA), modems, and speech recognition.

**Berlekamp–Massey (1968–1969).** Elwyn Berlekamp and James Massey. Efficiently finds the shortest linear feedback shift register generating a sequence — the key step in decoding Reed–Solomon and BCH codes (finding the error-locator polynomial).

**Gallager's LDPC codes (1962) and their 30-year sleep.** Robert Gallager, "Low-Density Parity-Check Codes," IRE Transactions, 1962 (his MIT PhD thesis). **The story:** LDPC codes — defined by a sparse parity-check matrix and decoded by iterative belief propagation on a bipartite (Tanner) graph — were computationally infeasible in 1962 and were **essentially forgotten for over 30 years**. They were rediscovered by **David MacKay and Radford Neal** (1996, "Near Shannon limit performance of low density parity check codes," *Electronics Letters*), who showed they approach the Shannon limit. Now used in Wi-Fi, 5G, and satellite. A parable about ideas born before their hardware.

**Turbo codes (1993).** Claude Berrou, Alain Glavieux, Punya Thitimajshima, "Near Shannon Limit Error-Correcting Coding and Decoding: Turbo-Codes," *ICC '93*, Geneva, pp. 1064–1070. **The story:** they claimed performance within a fraction of a dB of the Shannon limit — so close that, as Costello and Forney recount in "Channel Coding: The Road to Channel Capacity" (2006), after the 1993 Geneva talk *"comments to the effect of 'It can't be true; they must have made a 3 dB error' were widespread."* Within a year multiple labs confirmed it, launching the "turbo revolution." **The core:** two convolutional codes concatenated through a pseudorandom interleaver, decoded iteratively by exchanging soft "extrinsic" probabilities between the two decoders (turbo = feedback, like a turbocharger).

**Arıkan's polar codes (2009).** Erdal Arıkan, "Channel Polarization: A Method for Constructing Capacity-Achieving Codes for Symmetric Binary-Input Memoryless Channels," IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, 2009. **The core:** a recursive transform "polarizes" a set of channels into ones that are either nearly perfect or nearly useless; send data on the good ones, freeze the bad ones. The **first codes with a mathematical proof of achieving Shannon capacity** with efficient encoding/decoding. Adopted for 5G control channels — barely a decade from paper to global standard.

**Fountain / LT / Raptor codes (1998–2006).** Michael Luby (LT codes, 2002; earlier Tornado codes) and Amin Shokrollahi (Raptor codes). **The problem — the erasure channel:** packets aren't corrupted, they vanish (e.g., on the internet), and you don't have a back-channel to request retransmission. **The core:** a *rateless* code generates a limitless stream of random XOR combinations of source blocks; the receiver reconstructs the file once it has collected slightly more than k of *any* of them — like filling a bucket from a fountain, it doesn't matter which drops you catch.

### PART 5 — NAMING, LOOKUP AND PROBABILISTIC STRUCTURES

**DNS and Paul Mockapetris (1983).** Paul Mockapetris (at USC/ISI, at Jon Postel's request), RFCs 882 and 883 (November 1983), later superseded by RFCs 1034/1035 (1987). **The problem:** the entire internet's name-to-address mapping lived in a single flat file, HOSTS.TXT, maintained by SRI and copied everywhere — unscalable as the net grew. **The solution:** a hierarchical, delegated, distributed tree of names (. → .com → example.com), each zone administered independently, resolved by recursive/iterative query. He wrote the first implementation, "Jeeves." DNS is a graph (a tree) solving a naming problem at planetary scale.

**Bloom filter (1970).** Burton H. Bloom, "Space/Time Trade-offs in Hash Coding with Allowable Errors," *Communications of the ACM* 13(7), pp. 422–426. **The core:** a bit array plus k hash functions; to insert, set the k bits an element hashes to; to query, check those k bits. **False positives are possible; false negatives are impossible.** By accepting a small, tunable error rate, it uses a tiny fraction of the memory a real set would. Used in databases, web caches, and CDNs. A beautiful embodiment of "allowable errors" as an engineering resource.

**Consistent hashing (1997).** David Karger, Eric Lehman, Tom Leighton, Rina Panigrahy, Matthew Levine, Daniel Lewin, "Consistent Hashing and Random Trees: Distributed Caching Protocols for Relieving Hot Spots on the World Wide Web," *STOC '97*. **The problem:** ordinary hashing (server = hash(key) mod N) remaps almost *every* key when N changes — catastrophic when web-cache servers are added or fail. **The solution:** map both servers and keys onto a circle (hash ring); a key belongs to the next server clockwise. Adding/removing a server remaps only ~1/N of keys. This work became the technical core of **Akamai** — co-founder Daniel Lewin was tragically killed aboard the first plane to hit the World Trade Center on 9/11. Now the backbone of Dynamo, Cassandra, and memcached clusters.

**Chord and Kademlia DHTs (2001–2002).** *Chord* (Ion Stoica, Robert Morris, David Karger, Frans Kaashoek, Hari Balakrishnan, 2001) organizes nodes on a consistent-hashing ring with "finger tables" giving O(log N) lookup. *Kademlia* (Petar Maymounkov and David Mazières, 2002) uses an **XOR metric** as the distance between node IDs, giving an elegant symmetric routing structure; it powers BitTorrent's trackerless DHT and Ethereum's node discovery.

**HyperLogLog (2007).** Philippe Flajolet, Éric Fusy, Olivier Gandouet, Frédéric Meunier. **The problem:** count the number of *distinct* elements in a massive stream using tiny memory. **The core:** hash each element and track the maximum number of leading zeros seen; a run of k zeros suggests ~2ᵏ distinct elements. Averaging many such estimators (with harmonic mean and bias correction) is remarkably efficient — in the authors' own words, "HyperLogLog … is able to estimate cardinalities of > 10⁹ with a typical accuracy (standard error) of 2%, using 1.5 kB of memory."

**Count–Min sketch (2005).** Graham Cormode and S. Muthukrishnan. A 2-D array of counters with multiple hash functions estimates the frequency of any item in a stream in sublinear space, with one-sided error — the frequency analogue of the Bloom filter.

**Rendezvous / Highest Random Weight (HRW) hashing (1996–1997).** David Thaler and Chinya Ravishankar. Independently and roughly simultaneously with consistent hashing, it solves the same distributed-agreement problem: each client computes hash(key, server) for every server and picks the highest — agreeing on placement with no coordination and graceful remapping.

**Maglev hashing (2016).** Google's Maglev load balancer. A consistent-hashing variant optimized for near-perfect load evenness *and* minimal disruption, precomputing a lookup table for line-rate packet forwarding.

**Skip lists (1990).** William Pugh, "Skip Lists: A Probabilistic Alternative to Balanced Trees," *CACM*. **The core:** a linked list with randomized express lanes at multiple levels; search/insert/delete in O(log n) *expected* time, with far simpler code than balanced trees. Randomness replaces the bookkeeping of AVL/red-black rebalancing. Used in Redis sorted sets and LevelDB.

### PART 6 — DISTRIBUTED CONSENSUS AND TIME: agreeing when you can't trust the clock or the messenger

**The Two Generals Problem.** Two generals must coordinate an attack by messengers crossing hostile territory; any messenger may be captured. It is *provably impossible* to guarantee agreement over an unreliable channel — no finite number of acknowledgments ever makes both sides certain. This is the theoretical shadow over every TCP handshake: you can make agreement arbitrarily *probable* but never *certain*.

**Lamport, "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System" (1978).** Leslie Lamport, *Communications of the ACM* 21(7), pp. 558–565. **The core:** the **"happened-before"** relation (a partial order): events in one process are ordered; a send happens-before its receive. **Logical (Lamport) clocks** are per-process counters that respect this order (a → b ⟹ C(a) < C(b)), with ties broken by process ID for a total order. Explicitly inspired by the causal (light-cone) structure of **special relativity**. This is Lamport's most-cited paper — often cited as among the most-cited in all of computer science — and won the 2000 Dijkstra Prize. Its real subject, he notes, is the **state-machine replication** approach to fault tolerance.

**Vector clocks (1988).** Colin Fidge and Friedemann Mattern, independently. **The problem with Lamport clocks:** C(a) < C(b) does *not* imply a → b. **The fix:** each process keeps an N-vector, taking component-wise maxima on message receipt. Then **V(a) < V(b) ⟺ a → b exactly**, and incomparable vectors mean the events are genuinely concurrent. Causality captured precisely.

**The Byzantine Generals Problem (1982).** Leslie Lamport, Robert Shostak, Marshall Pease, "The Byzantine Generals Problem," *ACM TOPLAS* 4(3), pp. 382–401. **The core result:** if components can fail *arbitrarily* (send conflicting lies to different peers), consensus is achievable if and only if **more than two-thirds** of the participants are honest (can tolerate f faults with 3f+1 total). Lamport chose the "Byzantine" framing deliberately because an earlier "Albanian Generals" name risked offending a real nationality.

**FLP impossibility (1985).** Michael Fischer, Nancy Lynch, Michael Paterson, "Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process," *Journal of the ACM* 32(2), pp. 374–382. **The result:** in a fully asynchronous system, no deterministic algorithm can guarantee consensus if even a *single* process may crash — because a crashed process is indistinguishable from a slow one, so a correct process can never safely decide whether to wait or give up. Real systems escape this only via timeouts, randomization, or partial-synchrony assumptions. Won the Dijkstra Prize.

**Paxos and Lamport's Part-Time Parliament (1998).** Leslie Lamport, "The Part-Time Parliament," *ACM TOCS* 16(2), pp. 133–169. **The story:** Lamport cast his consensus algorithm as the reconstructed civic procedures of a fictional ancient Greek island, Paxos, and even lectured in an Indiana-Jones costume with a fedora and hip flask (an attempt at humor he later called "a dismal failure"). Reviewers demanded he remove "the Paxos stuff"; he refused, and the paper sat in a drawer for roughly eight years before publication. He later wrote the exasperated "Paxos Made Simple" (2001). **The core:** roles of proposers, acceptors, learners reach agreement on a single value across two phases (prepare/promise, then accept), safe despite crashes and message loss, using majority quorums and monotonically increasing proposal numbers.

**Raft (2014).** Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout, "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm," *USENIX ATC '14*, pp. 305–319 (Best Paper). **The motivation:** Paxos is notoriously hard to understand and implement. Raft is designed for *understandability*, decomposing consensus into **leader election, log replication, and safety**, with a strong leader and randomized election timeouts. A user study of 43 students found more learned Raft better than Paxos. It underlies etcd, Consul, and CockroachDB.

**CAP theorem (2000/2002).** Eric Brewer conjectured it as a keynote at PODC 2000; Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch proved it in 2002 ("Brewer's Conjecture and the Feasibility of Consistent, Available, Partition-Tolerant Web Services," *SIGACT News* 33(2)). **The result:** a distributed data store cannot simultaneously guarantee **Consistency, Availability, and Partition tolerance** — during a network partition you must sacrifice either consistency or availability. Brewer's 2012 retrospective clarifies that since partitions are unavoidable, the real design choice is C vs. A *during* a partition.

**CRDTs — Conflict-free Replicated Data Types (2011).** Marc Shapiro, Nuno Preguiça, Carlos Baquero, Marek Zawirski. Data structures (counters, sets, maps) whose merge operation is commutative, associative, and idempotent, so replicas that take concurrent updates **always converge** without coordination — the constructive, AP-side answer to CAP. Used in Redis, Riak, and collaborative editors.

**NTP and David Mills (1985).** David L. Mills (University of Delaware), Network Time Protocol, RFC 958 (September 1985, with companions 956/957; precursors back to RFC 778, 1981). Synchronizes clocks across variable-latency networks to within milliseconds of UTC, using a stratum hierarchy and statistical filtering of round-trip delays.

**Marzullo's algorithm (1984).** Keith Marzullo, Stanford PhD thesis, "Maintaining the Time in a Distributed System" (and Marzullo & Owicki, 1985). **The problem:** agree on the time given several clock sources each reported as a confidence interval, some possibly faulty. **The core:** return the smallest interval consistent with the largest number of sources (their intersection). A refined "intersection algorithm" is part of NTP's clock selection.

**Google TrueTime (2012).** James Corbett et al., "Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database," *OSDI '12*. **The counterintuitive move:** instead of pretending clocks are exact, TrueTime **exposes the uncertainty** as an interval [earliest, latest] with bound ε, guaranteed by GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every datacenter. To guarantee external consistency, a transaction performs **"commit wait"** — it deliberately *waits out* the uncertainty ε (typically a few ms) before making its commit visible, so timestamp order matches real-time order. Turning a hardware limitation into a first-class API.

**Chandy–Lamport snapshot algorithm (1985).** K. Mani Chandy and Leslie Lamport, "Distributed Snapshots: Determining Global States of Distributed Systems," *ACM TOCS*. **The problem:** capture a consistent global snapshot of a running distributed system with no shared clock and no stopping. **The core:** a process records its own state and sends a **marker** on every outgoing channel; markers delimit which in-flight messages belong to the snapshot. Foundational for checkpointing and stream processing (e.g., Apache Flink).

### PART 7 — COMPRESSION: squeezing out redundancy

**Huffman coding (1952).** David A. Huffman, "A Method for the Construction of Minimum-Redundancy Codes," *Proceedings of the IRE* 40(9), pp. 1098–1101. **The story:** in a 1951 MIT information-theory class, Robert Fano offered students a choice between a final exam and a term paper — find the most efficient binary code. Fano *did not tell them it was an open problem he and Shannon had failed to fully solve*. Huffman struggled for months, and just as he was about to give up and throw his notes in the trash to study for the exam, the solution — build the tree **bottom-up** from the least frequent symbols — came to him. His method provably beats his own professor's Shannon–Fano coding. **The core:** repeatedly merge the two lowest-probability nodes into a subtree; the resulting prefix code minimizes expected length.

**Lempel–Ziv LZ77 (1977) and LZ78 (1978).** Abraham Lempel and Jacob Ziv, two papers in IEEE Transactions on Information Theory. **The core:** instead of a probability model, exploit *repetition* — LZ77 replaces repeated strings with (distance, length) back-references into a sliding window; LZ78 builds an explicit dictionary of phrases. Dictionary compression that adapts to the data itself, requiring no prior statistics. The foundation of essentially all general-purpose compression.

**DEFLATE (Phil Katz, ~1990–1993).** Phil Katz (PKZIP), standardized in RFC 1951. Combines LZ77 with Huffman coding — the algorithm inside ZIP, gzip, and PNG. Katz's story is a poignant one: a brilliant programmer who died young in 2000.

**Arithmetic coding (1976–1979).** Jorma Rissanen and Richard Pasco (building on Elias). **The core:** encode an entire message as a single fractional number in [0,1), subdividing the interval by symbol probabilities. Unlike Huffman, it is not limited to an integer number of bits per symbol, so it gets arbitrarily close to the entropy bound — the basis of modern JPEG/H.264/H.265 entropy coders.

**Burrows–Wheeler transform (1994).** Michael Burrows and David Wheeler. **The counterintuitive core:** reversibly permute the data (sort all rotations of the block) so that identical characters cluster together, making the output far more compressible by simple follow-on coders. Reversible despite looking like it destroys information. The heart of **bzip2**.

**Brotli (2015) and Zstandard (2016).** Jyrki Alakuijala and Zoltán Szabadka (Brotli, Google — LZ77 + Huffman + context modeling + a built-in dictionary, used in HTTP compression and web fonts); Yann Collet (Zstandard, Facebook — LZ with finite-state entropy / asymmetric numeral systems by Jarek Duda, giving high ratios at very high speed).

### PART 8 — CRYPTOGRAPHY AND TRUST GRAPHS

**Diffie–Hellman(–Merkle) key exchange (1976).** Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, "New Directions in Cryptography," *IEEE Transactions on Information Theory* IT-22(6), Nov. 1976, pp. 644–654 — the first practical method to establish a shared secret over an insecure public channel. **The core:** agree publicly on a prime p and generator g; each side picks a private exponent, exchanges gᵃ mod p and gᵇ mod p, and both compute gᵃᵇ mod p. Security rests on the hardness of the **discrete logarithm** problem. **The Merkle story:** Ralph Merkle conceived public key distribution ("Merkle Puzzles," forcing an eavesdropper to do work growing as the square of the legitimate parties') as a 1974 project proposal in a Berkeley security course — which his instructor **rejected** as muddled; Merkle dropped the course but kept working, publishing "Secure Communications over Insecure Channels" in *CACM* (1978). Hellman later wrote, in "An Overview of Public Key Cryptography" (2002 reprint): *"While that system was first described in a paper by Diffie and me, it is a public key distribution system, a concept developed by Merkle, and hence should be called 'Diffie-Hellman-Merkle key exchange' if names are to be associated with it."* The now-expired US Patent 4,200,770 (from 1977) credits Hellman, Diffie, and Merkle as inventors.

**RSA (1977/1978).** Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, Leonard Adleman, "A Method for Obtaining Digital Signatures and Public-Key Cryptosystems," *CACM* 21(2), Feb. 1978 (MIT tech memo 1977). **The core:** n = pq for large secret primes; public exponent e, private d with ed ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)); security rests on the hardness of **factoring** n. **The story:** Rivest and Shamir kept proposing one-way functions and Adleman kept breaking them — reportedly dozens of times. On Passover night in April 1977, after wine, Rivest couldn't sleep, picked up a math textbook, and formalized the winning idea by daybreak. (Clifford Cocks at GCHQ had found an equivalent scheme in 1973, kept classified until 1997.)

**Merkle trees (1979).** Ralph Merkle, US Patent 4,309,569 (filed 1979). **The core:** a tree in which each leaf is a hash of a data block and each internal node hashes its children; the single **root** authenticates the entire set, and any item is verifiable via an O(log n) authentication path. The backbone of Git, Bitcoin/blockchains, and Certificate Transparency.

**The discrete log problem and elliptic-curve cryptography (1985).** Neal Koblitz and Victor Miller, independently. **The core:** replace modular arithmetic with the group of points on an elliptic curve over a finite field; the **Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem** has no known subexponential attack, so ECC achieves equivalent security with far smaller keys (256-bit ECC ≈ 3072-bit RSA). Koblitz and Miller shared the 2021 Levchin Prize.

**Hash chains.** Leslie Lamport again (1981): repeatedly hashing a secret produces a chain whose values can be revealed in reverse order as one-time passwords, since the hash is one-way — the ancestor of S/KEY and of many blockchain constructions.

### PART 9 — THE GENUINELY WEIRD FAILURE MODES (and the code that fixed them)

- **Count-to-infinity** — distance-vector loops incrementing hop counts toward infinity; fixed by split horizon, poison reverse, hold-down timers (Part 2).
- **BGP route flapping and route-flap damping** — a route oscillating up/down floods the internet with churn; damping suppresses a prefix that flaps repeatedly, with exponential penalty decay (RFC 2439).
- **The AS 7007 incident (25 April 1997)** — a misconfigured router at MAI Network Services (AS 7007) de-aggregated much of the global routing table into /24s and re-originated them, which BGP *preferred* because they were more specific. Traffic for much of the internet was black-holed into a ~45 Mbit/s link, and the routes persisted even after the offending router was disconnected. The lessons — filter your customers, don't redistribute BGP into your IGP — are still taught. This is the founding disaster of BGP security.
- **ARP storms / broadcast storms** — the loops Perlman's Spanning Tree Protocol was invented to prevent (Part 2).
- **The thundering herd** — many processes/connections all wake and contend for one resource at once (e.g., all cache entries expiring simultaneously); fixed by request coalescing, randomized jitter on timers, and exponential backoff.
- **TCP global synchronization** — flows halving windows in lockstep behind a tail-drop queue; fixed by RED's randomized early drop (Part 3).
- **TCP sequence-number prediction (Robert T. Morris, 1985)** — "A Weakness in the 4.2BSD UNIX TCP/IP Software" (Bell Labs Tech Report 117). Because initial sequence numbers incremented predictably, an off-path attacker could spoof a trusted host and inject packets without seeing replies; fixed by randomized ISNs (later formalized by Steve Bellovin). **SYN floods** exploit the half-open connection table; fixed by SYN cookies (Dan Bernstein / Eric Schenk).
- **The Morris worm (2 Nov 1988)** — Robert Tappan Morris (then a Cornell grad student; son of NSA cryptographer Robert Morris) released a self-replicating worm that exploited a fingerd buffer overflow, sendmail debug mode, and rsh trust. Per the FBI ("Morris Worm," fbi.gov), *"within 24 hours, an estimated 6,000 of the approximately 60,000 computers that were then connected to the Internet had been hit."* It led to the first felony conviction under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and to the founding of CERT.
- **Path MTU discovery black holes** — a host sets "don't fragment" and probes for the largest packet size, but if the needed ICMP "fragmentation needed" messages are filtered by a misconfigured firewall, large packets vanish silently and connections hang; fixed by PMTUD blackhole detection and packetization-layer PMTUD (RFC 4821).
- **TIME_WAIT exhaustion** — a busy server accumulates sockets stuck in the 2×MSL TIME_WAIT state (needed to absorb stray old packets), exhausting the ephemeral port space; mitigated by tuning, SO_REUSEADDR, and connection reuse.
- **The 500-mile email bug (~1994–2002, Trey Harris).** A statistics chairman reported he "couldn't send email more than 500 miles." True: a sendmail upgrade had reset the SMTP connection timeout to *zero*, so the effective timeout became a few milliseconds — and light travels only ~3 millilightseconds ≈ 558 miles in that time. The maximum email range really was governed by the speed of light. The canonical parable that "every impossible bug is rational — the gap is in your mental model, not in reality."
- **Leap-second bugs** — inserting a 61st second (23:59:60) to reconcile atomic time with Earth's rotation has repeatedly crashed systems that assume minutes have 60 seconds (notably a 2012 outage wave); mitigated by "leap smearing" (spreading the extra second over hours), pioneered by Google.
- **Clock skew and backward time jumps** — NTP correcting a fast clock can make time run *backward*, breaking code that assumes monotonicity; fixed by monotonic clocks (CLOCK_MONOTONIC) and slewing rather than stepping.
- **NAT traversal — STUN/TURN/ICE hole punching** — two hosts both behind NATs can't directly connect because neither has a public address. **STUN** discovers your public mapping; **TURN** relays as a fallback; **ICE** (Jonathan Rosenberg) systematically tries candidate pairs, "punching holes" by having both sides send simultaneously so each NAT sees outbound traffic first. The trick that makes peer-to-peer voice/video (WebRTC) possible.
- **Head-of-line blocking and why QUIC exists** — in HTTP/2 over a single TCP connection, one lost packet stalls *all* multiplexed streams because TCP must deliver bytes in order. **QUIC** (Jim Roskind, Google, ~2012; IETF RFC 9000, 2021) rebuilds transport over UDP with independent per-stream delivery, eliminating transport-level HOL blocking, and folds in TLS 1.3 for a 0/1-RTT handshake.
- **Head-of-line blocking in switches** — a packet at the front of an input queue destined for a busy output blocks packets behind it bound for idle outputs; fixed by **virtual output queueing** (per-output queues at each input).
- **The split-brain problem** — a network partition leaves two halves of a cluster each believing it is the sole master, both accepting writes; prevented by **quorum** (majority) requirements and fencing — the practical face of CAP and of Byzantine/consensus theory.
- **Slow-start restart** — after an idle period, a TCP connection's large congestion window is stale and may overwhelm a now-congested path; fixed by resetting to slow-start after idle (RFC 2861).
- **The "two generals" impossibility in TCP handshakes** — the three-way handshake makes connection setup arbitrarily reliable but, per the Two Generals result, never *certain*; this is why the final ACK can be lost and why protocols must tolerate ambiguity.

## Recommendations

For building the grimoire, I recommend the following staged structure and next steps:

1. **Anchor every entry to a primary source.** Each of the ~70 algorithms above has an original paper, RFC, or patent with a firm date; lead with that citation. For the pre-1970 items (Euler, Shannon, Hamming, Huffman, Reed–Solomon, Gallager, Viterbi, Dijkstra, Bellman–Ford, Ford–Fulkerson) the primary PDFs are freely available (Bell System Technical Journal archive, Numerische Mathematik, IRE/IEEE) and should be linked directly.

2. **Organize by the "two roots" spine you proposed.** Split the grimoire into a GRAPH-THEORY half (Parts 1, 2, 5, 6, and the routing/consensus failure modes) and an INFORMATION-THEORY half (Parts 3, 4, 7, 8). This makes the intellectual lineage — Euler→routing→consensus on one side, Shannon→coding→compression→crypto on the other — legible, and it directly serves your framing of "vertices/edges that don't quite exist" vs. "1s and 0s."

3. **Lead each entry with the failure, not the fix.** Your instinct is right: the count-to-infinity loop, the 40-bit/s collapse, the 500-mile email, and the AS 7007 black hole are what make the mathematics vivid. Structure every entry as (1) the weird failure, (2) the math, (3) the algorithm, (4) the human.

4. **Reconstruct pseudocode, never copy.** For Dijkstra, Bloom, Huffman, Paxos, AIMD, consistent hashing, and Viterbi, write fresh ~10-line pseudocode from the description — this avoids any copyright issue and forces clarity. (This report deliberately describes rather than reproduces code, per your note.)

5. **Prioritize the human stories that are documented verbatim.** The strongest, all sourced above, are: Dijkstra's café ("without pencil and paper"), Hamming's weekend ("Damn it, if the machine can detect an error…"), Huffman's trash-can epiphany, Perlman's Algorhyme, Lamport's Indiana-Jones Paxos lecture and ~8-year rejection, the turbo-codes "3 dB error" disbelief, Merkle's rejected class project, and Rivest's Passover night.

**Benchmarks that would change the emphasis:** if the goal is *breadth for a reference*, add the ~20 secondary items flagged lightly here (Tornado codes, BCH, Elias gamma, cuckoo hashing, gossip/SWIM, Merkle–Damgård, Schnorr signatures). If the goal is *depth for a narrative*, cut to the ~25 entries with the best human stories and full primary sources.

## Caveats

- **Dates and "invention" are often contested or dual.** Packet switching is jointly Baran (1964) and Davies (independently, mid-1960s); public-key crypto is Diffie–Hellman–Merkle (1976) with a classified GCHQ precursor (Cocks, 1973); ECC is Koblitz *and* Miller (1985); vector clocks are Fidge *and* Mattern (1988); consistent hashing (Karger et al., 1997) and rendezvous hashing (Thaler–Ravishankar, 1996) are near-simultaneous. I have credited all parties.
- **One primary-source discrepancy:** Jacobson & Karels' 1988 paper says LBL and UC Berkeley were separated by "400 yards and **three** IMP hops"; several secondary summaries say "two." The paper's own wording (three) should govern. The throughput figures (32 kbit/s → 40 bit/s, October 1986) are consistent across all sources including the paper itself.
- **The Hellman "Diffie–Hellman–Merkle" quote** appears in reprints dated 2002 and 2006; it is the same essay ("An Overview of Public Key Cryptography").
- **Some "stories" are lightly embellished in secondary retellings.** The Dijkstra, Huffman, Hamming, and Lamport-Paxos anecdotes are corroborated by primary interviews or the authors' own retrospectives and are reliable; the more dramatized blog versions (e.g., Perlman "waking in the night with the solution fully formed") should be treated as color, not fact — Perlman's own account is simply that it took her about a week.
- **A few attributions are conventional shorthand.** "Nagle's algorithm," "Karn's algorithm," etc. name the lead author of a collaborative effort; delayed ACK and Silly Window Syndrome trace to David Clark's RFC 813. DEFLATE is Katz's design but built on LZ77 (Ziv–Lempel) and Huffman.
- **This survey is broad rather than exhaustive.** Notable omissions include the full BCH code family, gossip/anti-entropy protocols (SWIM), Cuckoo hashing, the FFT's role in signal processing, and modern post-quantum lattice cryptography — candidates for a second volume.
